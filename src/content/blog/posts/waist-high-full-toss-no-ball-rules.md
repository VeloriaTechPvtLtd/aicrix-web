**A full toss above the striker's upright waist height at the popping crease is a no-ball. That does not make every such delivery an automatic dangerous-bowling warning.** Keep the height decision and the safety decision separate.

The reference position is an upright striker at the popping crease, even if the actual shot was played farther forward. Current MCC Law 41.7.1 and the October 2026 men's ODI conditions use that same reference. Neither provides a higher no-ball height allowance simply because the bowler is a spinner. [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), [ICC men's ODI conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf).

## Leaving the crease does not replace waist height with shoulder height

Imagine a batter stepping two metres down the pitch and meeting a descending full toss above the waist. The contact height alone does not settle the height no-ball. The relevant question is where the ball would have passed relative to that batter standing upright at the popping crease.

| Fictional observation | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| Contact above the waist well in front of the crease | More information is needed about the trajectory at the reference position |
| Projected passage below upright waist height at the crease | This particular height test is not met |
| Projected passage above upright waist height at the crease | The height no-ball test is met |
| Batter crouches as the ball arrives | Use the upright reference, not the lowered waist |

These examples do not turn a television still into a measurement. Perspective, the point of contact and the remaining flight path matter. Nor does passing this height test certify the entire delivery as legal: a separate infringement could still produce a no-ball.

## The warning asks a second question

MCC's safety assessment considers the delivery's speed, height and direction, the striker's skill and repetition, while disregarding protective equipment. A dangerous delivery or series brings a first-and-final warning; another dangerous delivery by that bowler in the innings brings suspension for the rest of the innings. The no-ball call is not conditional on that additional danger finding. [Law 41.7.2–4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

Consider three accidental full tosses in the same innings. For this invented sequence, assume all meet the height test, the first is judged not dangerous, and the next two are judged dangerous. No other warnings or infringements are involved.

| Delivery | Stated umpire assessment | Consequence in this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Height infringement, not dangerous | No-ball; no dangerous-full-toss warning yet |
| B | Height infringement and dangerous | No-ball; first-and-final warning |
| C | Another dangerous full toss | No-ball; bowler suspended for the innings |

The third row follows from the assumed findings, not from a universal rule that the third full toss is the limit. If A had already been judged dangerous, a further dangerous B could trigger suspension. Conversely, merely counting two no-ball signals does not reveal whether the required warning sequence occurred.

Neither the batter avoiding contact nor a ball missing the body proves that it was safe. The judgment concerns injury risk, not a requirement that someone actually be injured. A commentator's shorthand label, such as beamer, also does not replace the umpire's findings.

## Deliberate deliveries make the rulebook especially important

The deliberate-delivery provisions are not identical. MCC Law 41.7.6 bypasses the warning for a deliberately bowled unfair non-landing delivery and bars the bowler for the rest of the **match**. ICC men's ODI clause 41.7.4 addresses a deliberately bowled dangerous and unfair high delivery and bars the bowler for the rest of the **innings**. Do not silently exchange those sanction words. [MCC](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), [ICC ODI](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf).

That difference is particularly relevant when somebody quotes a general law to explain a competition ruling. Identify the competition and effective edition first. An ODI normally has one bowling innings per side, but that practical fact does not make the two documents' wording interchangeable.

In either discussion, immediate suspension means no prior warning is required under the applicable deliberate-delivery branch. It does not authorize a spectator to infer intent from an awkward release or a slow-motion replay. The examples here do not adjudicate a named incident.

## Why older explanations disagree

MCC announced in November 2018 that its full-toss sanctions would be adjusted from April 2019 to give umpires more discretion over which deliveries are dangerous. That historical change helps explain why an old two-beamer summary can omit an important distinction. The current text, not the age or popularity of a quotation, supplies the rule for the specified match. [MCC's announcement](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-announces-intended-changes-to-law-41-7).

## A practical checklist

Record four things before explaining a disputed full toss: the applicable rulebook; the projected height at the upright crease position; the danger finding; and any prior warning or finding of deliberateness. A score graphic showing only no-ball cannot supply all four.

A bounced short ball belongs to a different provision. The next-delivery free-hit question is also separate and depends on the competition's conditions. Check the applicable competition’s free-hit conditions rather than treating every cricket format as identical.
