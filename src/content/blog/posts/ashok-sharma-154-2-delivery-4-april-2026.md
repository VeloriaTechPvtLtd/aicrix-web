Ashok Sharma’s delivery to Dhruv Jurel on 4 April 2026 was reported at **154.2 km/h**, making it the fastest ball of that IPL season at the time. The same report describes the Gujarat Titans pacer delivering the yorker during the last ball of the 16th over against Rajasthan Royals in Ahmedabad. [Contemporary ANI report carried by The Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/ipl-2026/ashok-sharma-bowls-fastest-bowl-of-ipl-2026-vs-rajasthan-royals/).

That dated description matters. It identifies a particular ball and the lead reported that evening. It does not establish that the reading remained unbeaten through the completed tournament.

In scorecard notation, the final legal delivery of the 16th over is 15.6. “Sixteenth over” describes the innings stage, not Ashok’s sixteenth personal over.

The speed report and the wicket record also concern different events. Gujarat’s own match account identifies Ashok’s wicket as Shimron Hetmyer, caught by Mohammed Siraj. Jurel was dismissed later, in the final over, by Siraj. Do not combine the fast ball to Jurel with the wicket of Hetmyer to create a dismissal that did not happen. ## What happened in the match

Rajasthan made 210/6 and Gujarat finished on 204/8, a six-run defeat for the home side. The target was 211, so Gujarat finished seven short of the winning target but six behind Rajasthan’s total. Those two descriptions use different reference points. [Official IPL match report](https://www.iplt20.com/news/article/tata-ipl-2026-match-9-gt-vs-rr-match-report) and [Titans score summary](https://www.gujarattitansipl.com/news/gujarat-titans-vs-rajasthan-royals-tata-ipl-2026-match-report).

Ashok returned 1/37 in four overs. Dividing 37 by four gives an economy rate of 9.25. That describes the runs charged to his complete spell, not the speed or outcome of a single delivery. [Contemporary spell figures](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/ipl-2026/ashok-sharma-bowls-fastest-bowl-of-ipl-2026-vs-rajasthan-royals/).

A pace highlight and a spell summary therefore answer different questions. The highlight identifies a maximum reading; the bowling figures describe workload, runs conceded and wickets. Neither should replace the other in player analysis.

## Why an updated webpage can still contain an early table

CricTracker’s IPL speed page lists Ashok at 154.2 km/h, but the 2026 section explicitly says it was last updated on **11 May 2026**. A search result retrieved months later does not move that section’s coverage forward. [Dated speed table](https://www.crictracker.com/fastest-deliveries-in-ipl//).

This creates three separate dates to keep straight: the delivery on 4 April, the table’s stated update on 11 May, and the date someone opens the page. Only the first identifies the event. The second indicates the comparison’s stated freshness. The third proves neither that all subsequent matches were included nor that the list is complete.

Consequently, the supported claim here is the dated 154.2 km/h delivery and its reported early-season lead. A final-season ranking needs a complete end-of-season source before that stronger label belongs in the headline.

## What a complete speed claim would need

A single maximum cannot establish a spell average. The four-over spell contains 24 legal deliveries, whereas the highlighted reading describes only one. Without the other readings, there is no basis for calculating his mean pace, the proportion above a threshold or how his speed changed across the spell. A scorecard supplies balls and runs, not a missing speed distribution.

The same distinction applies to a season leaderboard. To call this the final fastest delivery would require coverage through the final match, not merely confirmation that the ball existed. A dated report can be accurate about an early lead while being insufficient for the later comparison. Keeping that limit visible protects the historical fact rather than weakening it.

## Use the number for the question it answers

For a fan revisiting the moment, retain the bowler, opponent, date and reading together. For a player comparison, add a defined sample rather than treating one maximum as every-ball pace. For a future match preview, the April reading does not establish current fitness, selection or bowling allocation.

The useful historical record is precise: Ashok, to Jurel, Gujarat against Rajasthan, Ahmedabad, 4 April 2026, reported at 154.2 km/h. It remains interesting without turning it into an unverified season-long or all-time claim.
