Being hit by a throw does not automatically make a batter out obstructing the field. Equally, being likely to reach the crease safely does not automatically protect a batter who deliberately obstructs the fielding side.

The key is to identify the relevant part of Law 37, not use one shortcut for every incident. The general rule concerns a wilful attempt to obstruct or distract by words or actions while the ball is in play; returning the live ball has its own specific consent requirement. [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

## Accidental contact is not the same as a deliberate route

The Laws exclude accidental obstruction and obstruction to avoid injury. The umpire must assess what happened. A fielder's belief that they were hindered is not, by itself, the whole legal test.

In its [30 April 2026 clarification](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/obstructing-the-field-ipl), MCC explains the importance of the batter's running line. It contrasts deliberately changing route into the line of a throw with continuing and returning along an existing line. The ball might hit the batter in either scene, but the sequence leading to contact changes the assessment.

Think of two hypothetical replays. In the first, a batter runs along one side, turns back on that same side and is accidentally struck. In the second, the batter deliberately changes course to place their body between the throw and wicket. An isolated frame of the impact would lose the distinction. Follow the path before deciding what the contact demonstrates.

## Must the throw have been certain to run the batter out

No. MCC's April explanation says that, outside the catch-prevention case, the likelihood of a dismissal is not a criterion for obstructing the field.

Therefore “the batter would have been home anyway” does not settle a deliberate-obstruction appeal. Nor should a writer manufacture a run-out probability to decide it. First establish the action and applicable provision; the exception concerning a prevented catch needs its own analysis. [MCC's clarification](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/obstructing-the-field-ipl)

## Ask before returning a live ball

Under Law 37.4, either batter can be dismissed for returning a ball still in play to a fielder with the bat or body without a fielder's consent. It is unsafe to assume that a helpful intention supplies permission.

MCC's [23 February 2026 explanation](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-clarification-on-obstructing-the-field) makes this a practical distinction. Interfering with the ball can affect the fielding side's options, including something happening at the other end that the batter has not noticed. The provision is not limited to cases where the batter admits an intention to cheat.

Compare another pair of invented situations: a fielder asks a batter to pass back the live ball, versus the batter scooping it up and returning it without being asked or receiving consent. Consent is the changed fact. Do not describe the two actions as equivalent simply because both look courteous.

The safe habit is to leave the live ball to the fielders unless consent has been given. A ball resting near the bat is not evidence, on its own, that every aspect of play has ended.

## An appeal and a decision are different steps

MCC's February clarification also separates the fielding side's choice whether to appeal from the umpire's adjudication once it does. Choosing not to appeal does not rewrite the underlying provision.

This helps explain why footage of a similar action with no dismissal may not establish a different law. Check whether an appeal occurred, whether the ball was still in play and whether consent was given, rather than assuming every unpenalized action created a precedent.

## Catch prevention needs special care

For a delivery that is not a no-ball, wilful obstruction by either batter preventing the striker being caught results in the striker's dismissal under Law 37.3. The 2026 scoring provision also lets the fielding captain determine the next striker in that catch-prevention case. This is not the ordinary crossing rule after a completed catch. [MCC Laws 37.3 and 37.5](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Do not turn that limited statement into a universal rule for no-balls or lawful second strikes in defence of the wicket. Those have express exceptions. For a real replay, establish the delivery's status and the exact action before selecting a clause.

An obstruction guide should clarify the decision, not declare a player dishonest from an edited clip. Preserve the official ruling separately from commentary about what a viewer thinks should have happened.
