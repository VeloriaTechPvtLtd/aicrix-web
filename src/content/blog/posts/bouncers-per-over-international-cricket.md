In men's international cricket, the allowance is **one fast short delivery per over in T20Is and two in Tests and ODIs**. A delivery can use that allowance even when it is called wide. The allowance belongs to the over, not to each batter who faces a ball.

That quick answer has two important limits. “Bouncer” is everyday language; the playing conditions use a height-based definition for a fast short delivery. And a numerical allowance does not give a bowler permission to bowl dangerously.

## The format comparison

| Format covered | Fast short deliveries allowed per over | First delivery above that allowance |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Men's T20 international | 1 | Second qualifying delivery is a no-ball |
| Men's Test | 2 | Third qualifying delivery is a no-ball |
| Men's one-day international | 2 | Third qualifying delivery is a no-ball |

These are the current ICC men's international conditions, effective October 2026: [T20I clause 41.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf), [Test clause 41.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf) and [ODI clause 41.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf).

Do not substitute a tournament's name with its broad format when checking a disputed incident. “T20” and “T20 international” are not interchangeable labels for the rulebook. For an IPL, school or club match, find that competition's conditions before applying this table.

## What height makes a delivery count

The Test definition uses the height of the striker's shoulders when standing upright at the popping crease. A qualifying ball passes, or would have passed, above that reference. The bowler's-end umpire advises the bowler and striker when one has been delivered. This means the batter's ducking position is not the reference height. [ICC Test conditions, 41.6.1.5–41.6.1.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

For a viewer, there are therefore two separate observations: where the batter actually moved, and the height at which the ball passed the rule's reference position. A low camera angle can make those observations difficult. A single still image of a crouching batter is not enough to reconstruct the umpire's assessment.

This also explains why a delivery described as “short” in commentary is not automatically a counted bouncer. Length describes where the ball landed. The allowance's definition adds a height test. Keep the descriptive word separate from the official classification.

## Does a high wide use the allowance

Yes. The men's T20I conditions explicitly say that a fast short delivery called wide under the high-delivery provision still uses the allowance. A high ball that prevents a normal cricket stroke is covered by that wide provision. Once the one-delivery T20I allowance is exceeded, the quota rule calls for a no-ball. [ICC T20I conditions, 41.6.1.7–41.6.1.8](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf)

The useful distinction is between an over's legal-ball progress and its short-delivery allowance. They are different counters. A scoring display that repeats a ball number after an extra does not, by itself, show that every other restriction has reset.

### A hypothetical T20I sequence

Imagine a bowler starts with a qualifying short delivery, called wide. On the next attempt the bowler delivers another qualifying fast short ball. It is tempting to say, “The first one did not count, so this is the first bouncer.” That reasoning confuses the counters: the first delivery used the short-ball allowance.

This is a constructed example, not a report of a match. Its purpose is to show why watching only the delivery number can produce the wrong conclusion. To audit a real incident, retain the umpire's calls and the delivery sequence together.

## Changing strike does not create a new allowance

The men's ODI rule sets two qualifying deliveries per over, not two per batter. Each further qualifying delivery in that over is a no-ball. An excess triggers a caution; a repeated instance in the innings brings a final warning, and a further instance leads to suspension for the innings. [ICC ODI conditions, 41.6.1.4 and 41.6.1.8–41.6.1.11](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf)

Consider an illustrative ODI over: Batter A faces the first qualifying short ball, then takes a single from a later delivery. Batter B faces the second qualifying short ball. The running ledger is two for the over, not one for each batter.

A helpful score-watching note would therefore be “two used this over.” Writing separate allowances beside each batter's name would create a false reset. The same bookkeeping principle applies when a new batter arrives after a wicket.

## The allowance is not a safety exemption

The MCC's general framework separately addresses dangerous short bowling. The umpire considers the striker's skill and the delivery's speed, length, height and direction; protective equipment does not remove the injury assessment. MCC Law 41.6 does not set the international table's fixed one-or-two allowance. It also addresses repeated above-head deliveries as unfair bowling. [MCC Laws, fourth edition 2026, Law 41.6](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

An argument that a bowler “still had a bouncer left” therefore cannot settle whether a delivery was dangerous. Counting and safety answer different questions. Nor should a viewer import one rulebook's warning sequence into another competition without checking its wording.

## A practical checklist for a disputed delivery

Start with the match identity and date. Then ask:

1. Which playing conditions govern this match?

2. Is the delivery being assessed under a fast-short-ball definition or a different no-ball rule?

3. What is the relevant upright height reference?

4. How many qualifying short deliveries have already occurred in this over?

5. Did a previous wide still consume the allowance?

6. Is the umpire making a separate dangerous-bowling assessment?

Record the facts before deciding that an umpire missed a call. A short clip can omit the earlier delivery that explains the decision. A rule summary without its format and effective date can be just as misleading.

## Common questions

**Does hitting a bouncer automatically stop it counting?** The international definition includes the height at which the ball would have passed. Bat contact alone is not a reason to assume that the allowance is restored.

**Is a waist-high full toss part of this comparison?** No. A non-landing delivery has a different rule. Check the full-toss provision rather than applying the short-ball allowance.

**Can I use the table for women's or domestic cricket?** Only after checking the relevant conditions. This guide deliberately identifies its men's international scope.

**What should I remember?** Keep three things separate: the competition's allowance, the delivery's classification, and the umpire's safety judgement. That is more reliable than remembering a bare number.
