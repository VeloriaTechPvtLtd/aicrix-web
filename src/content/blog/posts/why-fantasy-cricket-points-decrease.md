A fantasy total can fall even when a batter has not lost any runs. Some components are recalculated from the current innings, rather than permanently earned with each delivery. When a rate-based bonus drops, the new total can be lower than the previous snapshot.

That is one possible explanation, not a diagnosis of every discrepancy. To understand a particular change, compare the same player, match, mode and scoring version at two identifiable moments. Separate new contributions from changes to earlier adjustments.

## A dot ball can reduce a rate bonus

The [Dream11 T20 reference](https://www.dream11.com/features/how-to-play), checked on 14 November 2026, gives eligible players a four-point strike-rate adjustment from 150.01 to 170 and two points from 130 to 150. At least ten balls must be faced, and the adjustment excludes players classified as bowlers. These are the specific published bands used here, not universal rules for every format.

Imagine an eligible batter who actually starts a full-match T20 game, scores 17 from 11 balls, and has hit two fours and one six. Assume no fielding, bowling or other contributions, no captain multiplier and no source corrections. The boundaries account for 14 of the 17 runs; the other three runs are also included in the batting total.

| Snapshot | Runs and balls | Strike rate | All unchanged components | Rate adjustment | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before the dot | 17 from 11 | 154.55 | 35 | +4 | 39 |
| After the dot | 17 from 12 | 141.67 | 35 | +2 | 37 |

The unchanged 35 consists of 17 run points, eight for the two four bonuses, six for the six bonus and four for the qualifying starting appearance under the same published T20 reference. The dot adds no batter run or boundary. Its effect in this example is to move the innings into a lower rate band, reducing the calculated total by two.

No earlier batting run has been deleted. The four-point rate adjustment has been replaced by a two-point adjustment. These are calculated snapshots using the stated rules, not evidence that a particular app refreshes immediately after every delivery.

## A change is not always the points for the latest ball

A useful reconciliation separates three things: new event contributions, recalculated adjustments, and corrections to already recorded events. The difference between two totals is the net effect of all changes between those snapshots.

Continue the fictional innings with a single from the next ball. The batter now has 18 from 13, a rate of 138.46. The rate adjustment remains two. The other components rise from 35 to 36, so the total becomes 38. The latest change is +1, but the total is still one below the earlier 39.

| Interval | New run points | Change in rate adjustment | Net total change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 17 from 11 to 17 from 12 | 0 | −2 | −2 |
| 17 from 12 to 18 from 13 | +1 | 0 | +1 |
| Across both deliveries | +1 | −2 | −1 |

Looking only at the most recent scoring event would miss the earlier adjustment. The same issue arises when a reader compares screens captured several deliveries apart: the interval may include more than the last event shown.

## Equal totals do not imply equal recent increases

Consider two further invented displays, unrelated to the innings above. Player P moves from 40 to 50 points; Player Q moves from 46 to 50. Both end at 50, but their changes are +10 and +4 respectively.

There is no arithmetic conflict. A cumulative total and an increment answer different questions. Before comparing an arrow, badge or recent-change number with the main total, check what period that smaller number covers. Do not assume that every interface labels or updates these elements in the same way.

Similarly, compare base points with base points. A role multiplier or a different innings-only mode can make two displays incomparable before any live-update issue is considered. Establish that scope once, then inspect the changing components.

## Keep corrections separate from new play

A later correction can change a previously recorded contribution without a new delivery causing it. But a falling total alone does not prove that a correction occurred. Look for a revised event breakdown or source record instead of inventing a reason from the direction of the change.

Dream11’s published reference says live points can change while a match is in progress or awaiting review, and says no further adjustments are made after its completed status and winner declaration. That describes the provider’s stated finalization policy, not a measured update delay or a promise about another service.

For a fictional audit, suppose a component was recorded as eight points and is later corrected to zero. With all other entries fixed, that revision contributes −8 to the change. Record it as a correction to the earlier component, not as a negative contribution from an unrelated new ball. Whether such a revision is justified in a real case depends on the actual event and applicable rules.

## What to save when a difference remains unexplained

Keep both observation times, the match and mode, the player and listed role, the relevant scorecard state, and the component breakdown at each point. Calculate the later total minus the earlier total. Then check whether new contributions, recalculated bonuses and documented corrections account for that difference.

If only one total is available, the change cannot be reconstructed reliably. If the match is still under review, keep the result provisional. When a discrepancy remains, describe the exact unmatched component and timestamps rather than treating an unexplained number as proof of misconduct.

The key distinction is between an event and a running calculation. A delivery can add no batting runs yet change a rate-based adjustment; equal final totals can arrive through different increments. Comparing like-for-like snapshots makes those changes understandable without assuming either an error or a guaranteed explanation.
