Mohammed Shami’s 7/57 against New Zealand in the 2023 World Cup semifinal became India’s best men’s ODI innings figures. Before that, Stuart Binny’s 6/4 against Bangladesh in 2014 had overtaken Anil Kumble’s 6/12 against West Indies in 1993. These are historical milestones, not a claim that every later ODI has been checked. [ICC on Shami](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/icc-men-s-odi-cricketer-of-the-year-2023-nominees-revealed), [ICC on Binny](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/brilliant-binny-helps-india-defend-105)

## Why seven for 57 ranks above six for four

Bowling figures list wickets taken and runs conceded. In a best-figures ranking, wickets take priority. Shami’s extra wicket places 7/57 above either six-wicket spell even though he conceded more runs. When wickets are equal, fewer runs conceded ranks higher: Binny’s 6/4 therefore comes before Kumble’s 6/12.

That ordering is different from economy rate. It answers “which innings figures rank highest?”, not “who conceded runs most slowly?”.

## Binny’s spell in its match context

India were dismissed for 105 in a rain-shortened match on 17 June 2014. Binny took 6/4 from 4.4 overs, while Mohit Sharma took 4/22. Bangladesh were all out for 58 and India won by 47 runs. [ICC match report](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/brilliant-binny-helps-india-defend-105)

Here, 4.4 means four overs and four legal balls: **28 balls**, not 4.4 decimal overs. Binny’s economy rate was 4 × 6 ÷ 28, approximately **0.86 runs per over**. The arithmetic describes run prevention; the six wickets describe dismissals. Both were extraordinary, but they remain separate measures.

## Shami’s extra wicket changed the record

The ICC’s review identifies Shami’s semifinal seven-for as the best by an Indian in men’s ODIs at that point. His wickets included Kane Williamson, Tom Latham and Daryl Mitchell. [ICC retrospective](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/icc-men-s-odi-cricketer-of-the-year-2023-nominees-revealed)

A useful record entry should retain the opponent, year and figures together. Removing that context makes a statistical ordering look like a complete judgement of every spell, which it is not.
