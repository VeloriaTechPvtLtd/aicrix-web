---
title: "Post match dream team versus the actual winning team"
excerpt: "Compare a submitted fantasy team with a legal hindsight benchmark without confusing actual winners, captain changes or predictions."
status: draft
---

A post-match dream team and the winning entry need not be the same lineup. The first may be the highest-scoring legal selection anyone could have made. The second is an entry someone actually submitted. A pre-match suggested team is a third thing: it was chosen before the scores were known.

The label matters because each comparison answers a different question. A hindsight benchmark can show a scoring gap. It cannot, by itself, tell you your contest rank or prove that a prediction was good.

## First identify which comparison is on screen

A projected team is built before outcomes are known. A retrospective benchmark uses the scores that actually occurred. Both might be labelled “dream team”, but the label does not establish which calculation was performed.

Before comparing totals, retain the same fixture, player pool, scoring mode, credit prices, role restrictions and scoring snapshot. Preserve the original captain and vice-captain too. Quietly assigning the best scorers those roles rewrites the submitted entry rather than measures it.

## A small fictional example

Assume a teaching format with exactly three players: one keeper, one batter and one bowler, costing at most 25 credits. There are no other restrictions. The captain earns twice their base score and the vice-captain 1.5 times; they must be different selected players. All names, prices, points and entries below are invented. This is not a current Dream11 roster format.

| Player | Required role | Credits | Settled base points |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | Keeper | 9 | 60 |
| B | Keeper | 6 | 30 |
| C | Batter | 12 | 90 |
| D | Batter | 8 | 50 |
| E | Bowler | 11 | 80 |
| F | Bowler | 7 | 40 |

The highest scorer in each role gives A, C and E, but their combined cost is 32. That team fails the 25-credit limit. Its score is not an attainable benchmark under these fictional rules.

There are eight possible one-per-role rosters. Four exceed the budget; the four legal ones below exhaust the remaining choices. For each, the table assigns captain to its highest scorer and vice-captain to its next highest.

| Legal roster | Cost | Base total | Best retrospective total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A D F | 24 | 150 | 235 |
| B C F | 25 | 160 | 270 |
| B D E | 25 | 160 | 265 |
| B D F | 21 | 120 | 190 |

The legal hindsight maximum is 270 for B, C and F: 160 base points, plus an extra 90 for captain C and 20 for vice-captain F. B, D and E also total 160 before multipliers, but their best assignment reaches 265. Equal base totals do not necessarily produce equal final scores.

## Keep the original submission intact

Suppose the submitted roster was A, D and F, with F captain and D vice-captain. Its actual total is 150 + 40 + 25 = 215. Retrospectively changing captain to A while keeping D vice-captain raises the same roster to 235.

The 20-point difference is a role-assignment comparison within the original selection. Moving from that optimized 235 to the legal pool maximum of 270 adds another 35. Together they explain the 55-point gap from 215 to 270.

This decomposition follows a declared order: first reassign captaincy within the original roster, then change the roster and optimize again. It is an accounting comparison, not proof of two independent mistakes or a unique causal explanation. Changing the roster first can change how the intermediate gap is allocated.

## The winning entry comes from the submitted field

Now suppose exactly three entries were submitted: A D F with the original 215-point assignment, B D E with its 265-point assignment, and B D F with its 190-point assignment. The actual winner of this fictional field scores 265. Nobody submitted the possible 270-point lineup.

The original entry is therefore 50 points behind the winner but 55 behind the hindsight maximum. Calling both gaps the distance from first place would be wrong. If two submitted entries share the highest score, retain the tie unless the actual competition supplies a relevant tie-break.

Without the full submitted-entry results, a comparator cannot establish rank. Nor does a player-overlap count establish it: different captain assignments can produce different totals from identical selected players.

## Use settled points and draw a limited conclusion

[Dream11’s official scoring reference](https://www.dream11.com/features/how-to-play) says live points can change while a match is in progress or awaiting review, and that its completed status freezes adjustments. A comparison should retain the applicable platform’s settlement state rather than combine a final benchmark with an earlier live entry score.

A useful review preserves the original entry, its settled total, the legal benchmark and any separately available submitted-field result. Missing eligibility inputs should leave the benchmark incomplete, not be replaced by an unrestricted list of high scorers.

Finally, knowing the eventual best lineup does not show it was identifiable beforehand. Use the difference to ask which pre-match assumptions deserve checking; do not turn one hindsight result into a claim of forecasting skill or guaranteed future improvement.
