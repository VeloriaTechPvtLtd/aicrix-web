A clip of a bowler shining the ball can provoke two opposite comments: “Saliva is banned” and “They brought it back.” Both comments can refer to real rules while still giving the wrong answer for the match on screen. The missing information is the competition and the date.

This guide separates the international rule, the MCC framework and the IPL’s dated exception. It is checked as of 13 November 2026, not a promise that every tournament uses identical conditions.

## The short answer

Saliva is not a permitted polishing substance under the MCC’s October 2026 Laws. Those Laws allow sweat, subject to restrictions on how the ball is polished. The IPL, however, announced permission to use saliva effective from its 2025 season. A league-specific announcement does not establish what is allowed in another competition. [MCC Laws, Law 41.3](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf); [IPL advisory, 21 March 2025](https://www.iplt20.com/news/article/updates-and-changes-following-ipl-captains-meeting).

For a particular match, start with that competition’s applicable playing conditions rather than an undated search result.

## Why the ban began, and why old explanations conflict

In June 2020, the ICC introduced interim measures connected to cricket’s return during the COVID-19 pandemic. Its announcement prohibited saliva but described an adjustment period: initial leniency, up to two warnings per innings, and a five-run penalty for repeated use. It also instructed umpires to clean the ball when saliva was applied. This is the origin of many explanations still appearing in older articles and videos. [ICC interim regulations, 9 June 2020](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/interim-regulation-changes-approved).

In September 2022, the ICC announced that the prohibition would become permanent as part of changes effective on 1 October 2022. That announcement described a temporary measure becoming a lasting playing-condition change. It did not mean that every subsequent tournament document would have identical wording or procedures. [ICC changes, 20 September 2022](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/icc-announces-changes-to-playing-conditions).

The practical lesson is to separate the history of a rule from the version governing a match. An accurate report from 2020 can be an inaccurate guide to a 2026 incident.

## What the checked international conditions actually say

The ICC men’s T20I conditions effective October 2026 allow polishing on clothing with sweat as the only natural substance, without artificial substances or time-wasting. Clause 41.3.7 says that, when umpires believe saliva has been applied, five penalty runs go to the opposing side. The ball is replaced through the specified procedure unless the umpires believe the fielding team deliberately used saliva to obtain an advantageous ball change. [ICC men’s T20I conditions, clauses 41.3.2 and 41.3.7](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf).

That last qualification concerns replacement, not permission. “The ball stayed in use” is therefore not enough information to decide whether an action was lawful.

A hypothetical scoreboard illustrates the separate issues. If a batting side is 130/4 and receives five penalty runs, its total becomes 135/4. Whether a replacement is selected is another question. This is an arithmetic example, not a report of a real incident or a fantasy-points award.

Do not import the 2020 warning sequence into the dedicated clause above. Likewise, do not interpret a general ball-tampering warning provision as if it automatically overrode the saliva-specific instruction.

## Why MCC and ICC wording should not be mixed

MCC supplies cricket’s underlying Laws; governing bodies and competitions also use playing conditions. In the MCC 2026 unfair-alteration procedure, the opposing captain is asked whether they want the ball replaced, and five penalty runs apply regardless of that choice. That is not the same replacement process as the dedicated ICC clause just discussed. [MCC Laws, preface and Law 41.3.4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

When explaining a decision, identify one governing document and follow its process. Combining the penalty from one document, replacement choice from another and warnings from an older announcement produces a rule that may never have existed.

For recreational cricket, ask the organizer or umpire which local conditions apply before the match. Do not treat a televised league’s practice as permission for your own game.

## What changed in the IPL

The IPL’s official advisory on 21 March 2025 said that bowlers could use saliva to shine the ball effective from IPL 2025, following consultation with all ten teams. It described the change as a return to traditional practice after the COVID-era restriction. [Official IPL announcement](https://www.iplt20.com/news/article/updates-and-changes-following-ipl-captains-meeting).

That is a dated, competition-specific exception. It is evidence for what the IPL announced, not evidence that the ICC removed its international prohibition.

**2026 season boundary:** This guide has not independently verified the complete IPL 2026 playing conditions. The 2025 announcement should not be silently relabelled as a full audit of 2026 or 2027 rules. If evaluating a later-season incident, obtain that season’s conditions and any amendments first.

## A five-step check before sharing a controversial clip

1. **Identify the match.** Record the teams, competition and actual match date. A newly uploaded video may show an older game.

2. **Identify the applicable version.** The relevant document is the one governing that fixture, not necessarily the newest document available today.

3. **Separate observation from inference.** A hand near the mouth is not, by itself, a complete account of what happened to the ball.

4. **Check the umpire’s finding.** Report an official explanation when available. Do not turn a cropped replay into an accusation against a named player.

5. **Keep consequences separate.** Permission, a penalty, ball replacement and later disciplinary action are different questions. Explain only what the evidence establishes.

For example, imagine an unlabeled compilation containing an IPL clip and an international clip. Even if the physical action looks similar, one caption for both clips could mislead. Add the match identities and dates before drawing a rules comparison.

## What this means for cricket analysis

A rule about permitted ball care is not a ready-made forecast of wickets. If comparing performances before and after a rule change, first define the competition, seasons, match sample and other conditions being compared. Do not attribute every difference to saliva simply because the timing overlaps.

For fantasy research, use verified team roles, selection and the platform’s actual scoring rules. A five-run team penalty should not be converted into an assumed individual fantasy deduction without checking that scoring system.

## The takeaway

The useful question is not just “Is saliva banned?” It is “Which rules govern this match?” Check the date, competition and exact clause, then explain the sanction and ball-change decision separately. That approach resolves more confusion than treating one league’s announcement as a worldwide rule change.
