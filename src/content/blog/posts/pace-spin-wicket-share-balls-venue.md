---
title: "Pace versus spin at a venue and why wicket share can mislead"
excerpt: "Compare wicket share with wickets per legal ball, and see why bowling workload changes the conclusion."
status: draft
---

If pace takes 70% of a ground’s wickets, does that make it a pace-friendly venue? Not by itself. Pace may also have bowled most of the deliveries. A wicket share describes how the wickets were divided; a wicket rate relates those wickets to bowling opportunity. Both can be useful, but they answer different questions.

## Start with wickets and legal balls

Here is a hypothetical venue sample. All wickets in this first table are credited to the bowlers, every included delivery has a known style, and the figures cover the same competition, innings selection and date range.

| Bowling style | Credited wickets | Legal balls | Share of wickets | Wickets per 100 legal balls | Balls per wicket |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pace | 70 | 1,800 | 70% | 3.89 | 25.71 |
| Spin | 30 | 600 | 30% | 5.00 | 20.00 |
| Combined | 100 | 2,400 | 100% | 4.17 | 24.00 |

Pace supplied 75% of the legal deliveries and 70% of the wickets. Spin supplied fewer wickets overall, but more wickets per unit of bowling: 30 ÷ 600 × 100 = 5, compared with 70 ÷ 1,800 × 100 = 3.89. The larger wicket total therefore does not identify the higher observed rate.

[Minor League Cricket’s statistical guide](https://news.minorleaguecricket.com/2022/01/20/cricket-stats-101-part-two-bowling-statistics/) defines bowling strike rate as balls per wicket. In this example, spin’s lower 20-ball figure and higher five-wickets-per-100 figure describe the same relationship. These are aggregate historical rates, not a promise of a wicket on a particular delivery.

## Say which wickets form the denominator

Now extend the hypothetical table with ten more bowler-credited wickets whose bowling style is unknown. Pace’s share is 70% among the 100 classified wickets, but 70 ÷ 110 = 63.64% among all 110 credited wickets. Neither percentage is inherently wrong; the label determines whether it answers the stated question.

Unknown styles must remain visible. Do not silently distribute their wickets between pace and spin. If associated ball coverage is also missing, do not pair a complete wicket numerator with an incomplete ball denominator. A run-out is not a bowler-credited wicket, so a chart of all dismissals needs a separate category rather than adding it to a bowler’s tally.

## The rate still does not isolate the pitch

A comparison becomes more informative when it separates formats, competitions, innings and bowling phases. A powerplay-heavy pace sample and a middle-overs-heavy spin sample reflect different assignments as well as different bowling styles. The batters faced and the bowlers selected also differ. Dividing by balls corrects unequal workload; it does not remove all those differences.

Keep run prevention separate too. Wickets per ball cannot tell you runs conceded per over. Show both rather than folding them into an unexplained “friendly” label.

## A useful checklist for a pitch report

Before interpreting a pace-versus-spin chart, look for the match count and dates, the included competition and innings, credited wickets, legal-ball totals and unknown-style coverage. Check that percentages name their denominator and that rates can be reproduced from the displayed integers.

A small sample is still a small sample after normalization. If the chart cannot establish the underlying coverage, the honest conclusion is limited: it describes the supplied records. It cannot establish the condition of tomorrow’s surface or an individual player’s expected return.
