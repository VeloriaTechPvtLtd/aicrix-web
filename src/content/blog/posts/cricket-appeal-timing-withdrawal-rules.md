An umpire cannot give a batter out without a fielder’s appeal. But that does not mean every dismissal requires a visible raised finger: a batter who is genuinely out can leave the wicket without waiting for an appeal. [MCC Laws 31.1–31.2](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf) distinguish those two routes.

That distinction explains why “there was no appeal” and “the batter cannot be dismissed” are not interchangeable statements. It also helps separate three things often blurred in discussion: an ordinary appeal, an umpire recalling someone who left by mistake, and a captain withdrawing an appeal.

## The appeal window ends before the next delivery starts

Under [Law 31.3](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), the appeal must come before the bowler begins the next run-up, or the bowling action when there is no run-up, and before Time is called. It is not a deadline at the end of the next over.

The call of Over does not by itself close the window. A timely appeal can still be made before the following over starts if Time has not been called. Conversely, waiting until the bowler is already running in is too late even though the next ball has not left the hand.

Consider two hypothetical sequences after a possible catch on the sixth ball. In the first, Over is called, but the next bowler has not begun and Time has not been called. In the second, the next bowler has started the run-up. The score display may look identical, but the relevant timeline is different. Use the start of the next action, not the scoreboard’s over number.

## One appeal is not limited to the fielder’s guessed dismissal

An appeal of “How’s that?” covers all ways of being out. Officials answer matters within their respective jurisdictions; the [current men’s T20I conditions, clause 31](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf), preserve this framework. A player does not have to correctly diagnose every possible dismissal before asking for a decision.

That is separate from whether the evidence establishes a dismissal. Volume, confidence and the number of players appealing are not substitutes for the applicable facts. A replay should be assessed against the relevant rule rather than the enthusiasm of the appeal.

## Recalling a batter and withdrawing an appeal

The following distinction follows [ICC men’s T20I clauses 31.7–31.8](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf), matching the current MCC framework.

| Situation | Who acts and why | Relevant limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A batter not given out leaves under a reasonable mistaken belief of being out | Umpire signals dead ball and recalls the batter | Before the next ball comes into play; for the final wicket, before the umpires leave |
| Fielding side wishes to withdraw an appeal | Captain obtains the relevant umpire’s consent; the decision is revoked and batter recalled where applicable | Before the next ball comes into play; after a completed innings, before the umpires leave |

These are not two names for the same process. The first addresses a mistaken departure. The second addresses the fielding side’s appeal. Saying that a fielder looked apologetic does not establish that the captain and umpire completed a withdrawal.

## Ben Stokes’s recall was not a discretionary gift

In its [11 February 2019 explanation](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-law-change-benefits-ben-stokes), MCC described Stokes walking off after a return catch by Alzarri Joseph in St Lucia. The delivery was then identified as a no-ball. Stokes had not actually been given out and was recalled under the mistaken-departure provision.

MCC explained that crossing the boundary was no longer the decisive barrier after the 2017 change. Stokes was already approaching the pavilion, yet play had not advanced beyond the applicable recall limit. The episode demonstrates why “he has left the field” is an incomplete answer.

An umpire’s prompt correction, a recall and a player-requested review should not be casually merged. Identify which procedure the officials used before drawing a conclusion about what another match permits.

## Ian Bell’s 2011 reprieve was a different kind of case

The [ICC’s contemporary report](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/inside-none-of-us-felt-right-about-bell-incident-dhoni) describes Bell being given run out at Trent Bridge after leaving his crease believing tea had been called. India subsequently withdrew the appeal after discussions during the interval, and he resumed his innings.

The report treats the run-out decision as technically correct and the withdrawal as a separate decision. It is a historical example, not evidence that any captain can overturn any completed dismissal at any later time. Apply today’s procedural limits from the current document, not by copying an old headline.

## Keep appeals separate from DRS

An appeal asks the umpire for a dismissal decision. A Player Review challenges an eligible decision under a competition’s review protocol. This guide does not replace the review timer or allowance rules.

Before explaining a disputed incident, identify the original decision, any mistaken departure, the captain’s request if one occurred, the umpire’s response, and the next-delivery timeline. If a clip does not show those stages, describe the missing evidence rather than inventing a completed withdrawal.
