**A shortened men’s T20I does not automatically leave each bowler with four overs.** The revised innings length determines the limit. A second distinction matters when someone cannot finish an over: scorecard workload and quota used are not always the same quantity.

## Start with the applicable innings allocation

Under [ICC men’s T20I conditions effective October 2026](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf), clauses 13.9.1–13.9.3, the ordinary ceiling is four overs. For rescheduled innings of at least ten overs, the rule uses one-fifth of the allocation, with extra whole overs assigned as necessary for the remainder. At five to nine overs, the ceiling is two.

These are international conditions, not a universal rule for every competition called T20. Also, a maximum is permission to bowl up to a limit, not a promise that a player will receive that workload.

## Distribute the remainder instead of rounding everyone up

Here is a hypothetical thirteen-over innings arranged before play. Five bowlers taking two overs each cover ten. Three more overs remain, so three can receive one additional over. One complete distribution is:

| Bowler | Scheduled overs |
| --- | ---: |
| A | 3 |
| B | 3 |
| C | 3 |
| D | 2 |
| E | 2 |
| Total | 13 |

The arithmetic is 13 = 5 × 2 + 3. It is not a 2.6-over allowance expressed in cricket notation. Giving all five bowlers three overs would create fifteen overs of assumed capacity, exceeding this remainder distribution.

A feasible hypothetical order is A, B, C, D, E, A, B, C, D, E, A, B, C. It totals thirteen without consecutive overs by one bowler. The example is an allocation demonstration, not a recommendation about which players should bowl which phases.

## Eight overs does not force a four-bowler attack

In a fictional eight-over innings, 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 is one possible allocation. So is 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1. Both total eight and keep every individual within two.

A one-over return therefore does not establish a one-over rule. Store the permitted limit and actual allocation separately.

## When two bowlers share one over

ICC clause 13.9.4 counts a replacement’s part-over as a full over for each bowler’s individual limit. Clauses 17.6–17.8 also prevent bowling consecutive overs or parts of consecutive overs and govern finishing an over after incapacity or suspension. [Current conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf).

Take a hypothetical uninterrupted twenty-over innings. A has completed three overs and delivers two legal balls of a fourth before becoming unable to continue. B, who has not bowled earlier, completes the remaining four balls.

| Item | A | B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actual legal balls delivered so far | 20 | 4 |
| Scorecard overs notation | 3.2 | 0.4 |
| Overs consumed for the individual limit | 4 | 1 |

The split contributes six legal balls to the team innings, not twelve. Yet the quota ledger charges one over to each participant for that shared over. A has no quota left in this example; B can have three more full overs within the four-over ceiling, subject to the other eligibility rules.

If B later bowls those eighteen legal balls, B’s actual figures reach 22 balls, or 3.4 overs, while the quota ledger reaches four. There is no missing pair of deliveries to reclaim. The two ledgers answer different questions.

## Keep the restart decision separate from a calculation

After an interruption, use the officials’ revised allocation and the record of overs already delivered. Do not treat a reduction as a fresh innings that erases previous bowling. A complicated mid-over reduction needs the applicable conditions and an umpire’s ruling, not an improvised reset.

For a reader, the useful fields are competition and version, revised innings length, each bowler’s actual delivery count, any shared over and remaining permitted allocation. Powerplay placement and a rain-adjusted target are separate checks. None of these fields establishes who will actually bowl next.
