A ball leaving the ground does not automatically entitle the fielding team to a brand-new one. Replacing missing equipment and exercising a new-ball option are different decisions. The important questions are why the ball is being changed, which competition rules apply, and how much play has taken place with it.

## When a replacement is required

Under [MCC Law 4.5](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), the umpires replace a ball that cannot be found or recovered, or that they agree has become unfit through normal use. The replacement should have comparable wear. They notify the batters and fielding captain.

That is not the same as changing a ball simply because a bowler dislikes its behaviour. Nor does a six by itself tell viewers whether replacement will be necessary: a ball that can be recovered is a different situation from one that cannot.

## The Test distinction between 80 and 110 overs

The [ICC men’s Test conditions effective October 2026](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf), clauses 4.4–4.5, retain a fielding captain’s option to demand a new ball after at least 80 completed overs with the old one; part overs are excluded. Separately, if replacement is needed after 110 overs because the ball is lost, unrecoverable or unfit through normal use, the replacement is new. That is not an instruction to discard a playable ball automatically.

## Three hypothetical situations

These examples assume the original ball has remained in use throughout. They illustrate different triggers, not events from an actual match.

| Situation | Decision |
|---|---|
| Original ball lost after 35 completed overs | Comparable-wear replacement under the ordinary rule. |
| Original ball still playable after 80 completed overs | The fielding captain may choose the new ball. |
| Original ball lost after 111 completed overs in an ICC men’s Test | The conditional replacement is a new ball. |

An over display needs careful reading. At 79.4, there are 79 completed overs and four further legal deliveries, not 79.4 decimal overs. Two more legal balls complete the 80th over. For this uninterrupted-ball example, 79 × 6 + 4 = 478 legal deliveries, compared with 480 after 80 complete overs. Counting an incomplete over as complete would make the option appear available too early.

## How to read a ball change in commentary

Record the reason and the time of the change separately. A useful note might say “ball unrecoverable; replacement introduced” rather than merely “new ball”. The latter description could wrongly suggest the fielding captain exercised an option, even when officials were replacing lost equipment.

Keep the ball’s use history alongside the innings over number. Those figures coincide in the examples above because no earlier change occurred. They should not be assumed identical in every innings. A broadcast image of a replacement ball alone cannot establish the reason for its introduction.

Finally, a rule about replacement does not predict the next delivery. Whether the ball will swing more, produce a wicket or change scoring is a separate cricket question. Read the applicable conditions before applying this men’s Test exception to another competition; ODI ball allocation and pink-ball selection answer different questions.
