**A missed stop is not automatically fake fielding.** The important question is whether the fielder deliberately tried to deceive, distract or obstruct a batter, and what the umpire decided.

[MCC Law 41.5 in the 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf) concerns conduct after the striker receives the ball. Either umpire judges whether it was wilful. For an ordinary non-contact offence, the ball becomes dead, neither batter can be dismissed from it, five penalty runs are awarded and it does not count in the over. Completed runs and the run in progress count; the batters choose who faces next. Applicable No-ball or Wide calls remain. Physical contact requires the additional Law 42 assessment.

That is more specific than “the fielder looked as though they had the ball.” A replay can help describe an action, but the scorebook depends on the ruling, not a viewer’s impression.

## A fake throw that was penalized

On 29 September 2017, Queensland’s Marnus Labuschagne missed a diving stop in the one-day match against the Cricket Australia XI, then got up and shaped to throw. Param Uppal reacted before recognizing that the ball had continued towards another fielder. The umpires awarded five penalty runs, as [Cricket Australia’s contemporary report](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/3282151) records.

The sequence matters: the failed attempt to stop the ball was followed by a separate throwing gesture without the ball. Describing only the dive would omit the conduct central to that ruling. It is a dated example of enforcement, not proof that every unsuccessful dive deserves a penalty.

## A slide that was not penalized

George Bailey’s February 2019 BBL incident had a different outcome. He slid while teammate Ben McDermott collected the ball and threw it; the umpires found no wilful attempt to deceive. [Cricket Australia’s officiating department supported the non-call](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/3304263/ca-approves-fake-fielding-non-call). Its explanation distinguished a genuine fielding action from a deliberately misleading one.

The two examples are useful together because they prevent an over-simple visual rule such as “a slide without possession equals five runs.” Position, sequence and the officials’ assessment matter. Reusing the Labuschagne result as an automatic verdict on another player would skip those facts.

## A hypothetical scorebook example

Hypothetical example: at 100/2 after 10.2 overs, the striker hits a delivery, completes one run and starts another before an ordinary non-contact offence. Assume no other extras, penalties, boundary or short run.

| Component | Added runs |
| --- | --- |
| Completed run | 1 |
| Run in progress | 1 |
| Penalty award | 5 |
| Total increase | 7 |

The total becomes 107/2 at 10.2 overs. The striker receives two runs; five are penalty runs. Neither “105/2” nor “10.3 overs” describes this example.

This clean scenario is deliberately limited. Adding other events requires a fresh calculation, not a silent change to the assumptions.

## Keep an allegation separate from an official result

A useful match record should distinguish a suspected incident, the umpire’s ruling and the credited score. An article or social post saying “that should have been five” does not itself create an official five-run award. Do not rewrite a historical result by adding hypothetical penalty runs and presenting the new total as the match result.

The same separation helps fantasy readers. Team runs, batter runs and a platform’s points are three different records. Check the applicable scoring rules before translating any penalty into an individual fantasy total; the sporting sanction alone does not supply that calculation.
