A ball stuck in clothing is not automatically a dead ball, and a ball touching a helmet is not automatically five penalty runs. Start with whose equipment it is, whether it is being worn and what actually happened: a rebound, genuine trapping or deliberate use of clothing.

This distinction matters because a keeper reaching for a ball lodged in a batter’s pad is not doing the same thing as securing an edge that rebounds from the keeper’s own worn helmet. The object alone cannot supply the decision.

The baseline here is the [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), checked against the [ICC men’s Test conditions effective October 2026](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf). The examples concern ordinary deliveries with bat contact, no no-ball, no ground or boundary contact, and no earlier dismissal or safety stoppage. Competition-specific head-impact instructions must also be checked.

## A batter trapping the ball ends that passage of play

MCC Law 20.1.1.4–5 makes the ball dead when it becomes trapped between a batter’s bat and person or between items of batting clothing or equipment, or lodges in that clothing or equipment. Lodging in an umpire’s clothing is also covered. This is different from merely brushing or rebounding from an item. [MCC Law 20](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Imagine an inside edge becoming genuinely wedged in the top of the striker’s pad. A keeper then removes it and holds it up. The later possession cannot create a catch from a ball already dead. Change the fact to a ball simply rebounding from the pad without being trapped, and that automatic stopping event has not been established.

The useful replay question is therefore whether the ball lodged or remained in motion through ordinary contact, not whether the keeper eventually held it. This example assumes trapping is clear; an unclear camera angle is not proof of either version.

## Fielding clothing has a different route to a catch

ICC clause 33.2.2.1 permits a catch involving accidental lodging in a fielder’s clothing or lodging in worn external protective equipment. But clause 33.3 still requires complete control of the ball and the fielder’s movement. Equipment contact does not remove the other catch requirements. [ICC clauses 33.1–33.3](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

Consider a close fielder whose loose shirt accidentally receives an edge. If the ball remains secured and the fielder achieves the required control, the lack of a conventional two-handed grip does not itself defeat the catch. If the ball falls out onto the ground before completion, the same appealing photograph taken a moment earlier cannot establish a completed catch.

## Compare the sequences rather than the objects

| Fictional sequence | Decision path |
| --- | --- |
| Edge becomes wedged in the batter’s pad, then keeper retrieves it | Ball already dead; retrieval cannot create a catch |
| Edge accidentally lodges in a fielder’s shirt and remains secured through complete control | Can satisfy the catch requirements |
| Edge rebounds from the keeper’s worn helmet into gloves, then full control | Helmet contact alone does not invalidate the catch under the stated baseline |
| Fielder stretches the shirt with both hands to scoop the ball | Apply illegal-fielding rules, not accidental-lodging permission |

These are separate hypothetical events. They are not four stages of one delivery or judgments about identifiable players. The third row also depends on the safety qualification below; it is not an instruction to ignore a head impact.

Deliberately extending clothing by hand to field the ball is prohibited by ICC clause 28.2.1.2. The word “accidentally” in the clothing catch provision matters: it is not permission to turn a shirt into a catching net. [ICC clause 28.2](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

## Why a local match may need a different answer

The [ECB ACO clarification dated 3 May 2024](https://www.kentcricket.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/ECB-ACO-Concussion-Guidance-with-Clarification-May-2024.pdf) gives a concrete example of a safety instruction changing this analysis in recreational cricket. It directs immediate dead ball for a head strike, including a glancing helmet impact, and says a subsequent catch cannot dismiss the batter. That document is a dated governing-body example, not a claim that every competition uses the same procedure.

Before applying the keeper-helmet example to a club match, check the competition’s current head-impact instructions. Do not infer them from a televised international or from an old answer online. A catch interpretation is also not a medical assessment of whether a player can continue.

## What to establish before deciding

Record the person involved, the equipment’s worn or loose status, the contact sequence and any applicable safety stoppage. For a potential catch, follow the sequence through control rather than stopping at first contact. For trapping in batting equipment, identify the earlier event that ended play.

A helmet stored on the ground belongs to a separate penalty rule. Similarly, whether a dead delivery counts in the over is a separate scoring question. Neither needs to be guessed to answer the narrower question here: was this still a live ball capable of becoming a fair catch?
