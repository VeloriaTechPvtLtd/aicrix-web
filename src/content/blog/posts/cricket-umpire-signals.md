Cricket umpire signals are easiest to learn in groups: arms held out, hands raised, and gestures around the shoulders. Watching the complete movement matters. A frozen image can hide the difference between a repeated tap and a hand simply resting in place.

Start with the gesture, then check the scoring consequence. Recognising a signal is useful, but it does not necessarily explain every detail of the delivery.

## The everyday signals

This quick reference follows MCC Law 2.13 in the October 2026 edition.

| What you see | Signal |
| --- | --- |
| One arm stretched sideways at shoulder level | No ball |
| Both arms stretched sideways | Wide |
| Index finger lifted above the head | Out |
| Wrists crossing repeatedly below the waist | Dead ball |
| An arm sweeping sideways and finishing across the chest | Boundary four |
| Both arms lifted overhead | Boundary six |
| One open hand held overhead | Bye |
| A hand touching a lifted knee | Leg bye |
| The ball held above the head | New ball |

[MCC Laws of Cricket 2026, Law 2.13](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

For learning, contrast neighbouring shapes rather than memorising an uninterrupted list. Look for one arm versus two, sideways versus overhead, and an empty hand versus a hand holding the ball. Those are useful observation prompts when a broadcast briefly cuts to the umpire.

## Four shoulder gestures that mean different things

The October 2026 ICC men's Test conditions distinguish these closely related movements:

- Repeatedly tapping the opposite shoulder means five penalty runs to the batting side.

- Holding a hand on the opposite shoulder means five penalty runs to the fielding side.

- Bending an arm and bringing the fingertips to the nearer shoulder indicates a short run.

- Touching both shoulders with the opposite hands revokes the last signal.

The same conditions require separate scorer acknowledgement and place multiple signals in the order of the events. [ICC men's Test conditions, clause 2.13](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf).

A useful viewing habit is to ask three questions: Which shoulder? One hand or both? Was the hand tapping or stationary? Avoid labelling every shoulder movement “penalty runs”. If you missed the movement, wait for the scoring update rather than treating the first plausible interpretation as certain.

## Why one delivery can produce more than one gesture

The signal sequence communicates more than a single headline. Under MCC Law 2.13, live-play signals other than Out are repeated to the scorers after the ball becomes dead. That repetition is communication, not evidence of another delivery. [MCC signal sequence](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

Imagine a fictional broadcast showing a no-ball signal and later a boundary-four signal. Your first task is to recognise two different pieces of information, not choose one and discard the other. Then check the official score entry for how the delivery was recorded. This example illustrates how to watch a sequence; it is not a substitute for the detailed scoring rules.

If the camera returns to the crowd between gestures, you may not have seen the whole sequence. A missing television shot is not evidence that the umpire omitted a signal.

## What a signal does not tell you

An Out signal identifies the decision, not the dismissal type. A raised finger alone cannot distinguish a caught decision from an lbw decision. [MCC Law 2.13](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

Apply the same care when summarising a clip. Describe what is established, then check the scorecard or official explanation for the missing detail. Do not add the reason for a penalty, the identity of a dismissed batter or a complete scoring calculation just because you recognise one gesture.

For a beginner keeping practice notes, separate three columns: observed movement, apparent signal, confirmed score entry. This makes it easier to spot whether an error came from misreading the gesture or misunderstanding the scoring rule.

## A simple way to practise

Watch a short passage with the commentary muted. Write down the movement before naming it, then compare your notes with the official scoring update. Rewatch any shoulder gesture at normal speed as well as paused: timing can be part of the distinction.

Treat this as a core reference rather than a catalogue of every signal used in every tournament. When an unfamiliar competition-specific gesture appears, check that competition's current playing conditions. Learning the familiar shapes is the starting point; reading the whole sequence is what makes them useful.
