A stop clock is not an instruction to finish an entire over in one minute. It regulates the gap between overs. Understanding that distinction explains why a team can face a five-run sanction even when no delivery has been bowled during the delay.

This guide separates the fielding-side clock from batting delays and the broader question of slow over rates. Its detailed Test examples use the ICC World Test Championship conditions effective from October 2026, rather than assuming every domestic competition uses identical rules.

## The warning sequence and the Test reset

The ICC’s Test stop-clock framework uses a 60-second interval, two warnings and five penalty runs for a third or further breach. It also specified a reset after each completed block of 80 overs in an innings. [ICC announcement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/several-changes-made-to-icc-playing-conditions)

Consider a fictional innings with warnings after overs 21 and 47. A further qualifying breach before the 80-over reset brings five runs. Changing the bowler does not turn this into a fresh team history. After 80 overs are completed, the warning count starts again. Think of separate 80-over accounting blocks, not a lifetime tally across the whole Test.

The reset is easy to confuse with a tactical decision about taking the new ball. Keep the two questions separate: one concerns the warning ledger; the other concerns the ball the captain wants to use. A useful broadcast graphic should show the relevant block and warnings, not just a red timer.

## When the clock actually runs

Under clause 41.9.4, the third umpire starts timing after the previous over's final ball is dead or its review has finished, whichever is later. The fielding side must start the next over within 60 seconds unless the umpires or batters prevent it. Exceptions include an incoming batter, official drinks, approved injury treatment and delays beyond the fielding side's control. These are not automatic violations simply because a viewer's stopwatch has passed one minute. [October 2026 WTC conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/jrwhpcwk8mmpv0fx3lnd.pdf)

For someone following a live stream, the practical lesson is to distinguish elapsed broadcast time from the official clock. Replays, commentary and an unresolved review can make a pause look long without establishing an offence. Record what the umpires announce before diagnosing an error from the television pictures.

## The USA example from the 2024 World Cup

USA received a five-run stop-clock penalty against India in New York on 12 June 2024 after a third violation. India were 76 for three and needed 35 from the last five overs before the award. Those five runs reduced the remaining requirement to 30, without a boundary or a completed run. [ICC match explainer](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/what-is-a-stop-clock-penalty-and-why-were-usa-punished-against-india)

That is a useful illustration of the rule's immediate scoreboard effect, not proof that the sanction alone decided the match. A match contains many earlier opportunities and later decisions. Saying the penalty changed the equation is precise; saying it mechanically caused the final result goes further than the example establishes.

## Batting delays are a different responsibility

The October 2026 changes also prescribe a warning, final warning and then five runs to the fielding side for repeated batting readiness offences. The changes apply to series beginning on or after 1 October, with ongoing series retaining their previous regulations. Do not fold those batting warnings into the fielding-side stop-clock tally. [ICC October update](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/key-changes-across-formats-with-new-icc-playing-conditions)

Nor should a short clip become a substitute for checking the competition and date. A franchise league's advertised two-way clock may have its own wording. Similar-looking graphics do not establish identical regulations.

## A reliable way to follow the decision

When a timer becomes a talking point, establish the match's playing conditions, the responsible side, the official warning history and any recognised interruption. Then separate the sanction from your opinion of its tactical importance.

For scorekeeping analysis, preserve the event as a penalty rather than inventing a shot to explain the score jump. For fantasy analysis, do not allocate a team penalty to a batter simply because that batter is on strike. Check the platform's scoring rules independently.

The clock is best understood as a specific between-overs control. It is not a universal stopwatch for every pause, a substitute for all over-rate regulations, or a reason to guess what officials have recorded.
