India reached **118/0 after six overs** against New Zealand in Wellington on 27 October 2026. The opening burst by Abhishek Sharma and Vaibhav Sooryavanshi put the pursuit of 199 on a very different footing: only 81 more runs were needed from the remaining 84 legal balls. [ICC match report](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/india-race-to-a-historic-chase-to-seal-t20i-series-in-wellington)

Comparing that start with other extraordinary men's T20I powerplays requires more than sorting a column of runs. A chase can finish before six overs, two innings can share the same run total with different wickets lost, and an opening partnership can continue beyond the restricted-fielding phase.

## Why 116 in 5.4 overs is not a six over score

Romania chased Serbia's 115/7 without losing a wicket. Their 116 arrived in **5.4 overs**, which means 34 legal deliveries, not 5.4 decimal overs. The game ended then, so the score cannot be extrapolated into an observed six-over total. [Complete Romania innings](https://www.cricbuzz.com/live-cricket-scorecard/100623/srb-vs-rom-1st-semi-final-sofia-t20-2021)

The distinction changes a rate comparison. India's 118 from 36 balls is 19.67 runs per over. Romania's 116 from 34 is 20.47. India scored more runs in the displayed phase, while Romania scored faster over its shorter completed chase. These statements are compatible because total runs and runs per over measure different things.

Projecting Romania's rate across two unused balls would create an invented score. Conversely, excluding it without explaining the completed-chase rule would hide why some published lists differ.

## The Wellington partnership was longer than the powerplay

The ICC records a **133-run opening partnership from 43 balls** in the Wellington chase. That is not an alternative value for the 118-run powerplay: the partnership continued for seven more legal balls. Its additional runs were 133−118 = 15.

After six overs, the required rate was 81 ÷ 84 × 6 = **5.79 per over**. That shows how far the opening burst reduced the asking rate. It does not mean the remaining runs were automatic, or that the whole 133-run stand belongs in an opening-phase statistic.

India eventually won by six wickets after 13.2 overs. A phase comparison should retain its six-over cutoff rather than replace it with the final chase rate or Abhishek's full-innings score. ## Read record lists with their coverage attached

A list titled “100+ powerplay scores” can still miss an eligible innings. The [27 October Wisden list](https://www.wisden.com/series/india-vs-new-zealand-m-2026/cricket-news/highest-powerplay-score-in-mens-t20is-full-list-india-demolish-new-zealand-to-break-world-record) contains eight entries but omits Eswatini's 106/1. The [7 June final scorecard](https://www.cricbuzz.com/live-cricket-scorecard/161465/eswatini-vs-mozambique) records 106 runs in the mandatory phase, with the first wicket at 5.1 and the second at 9.1.

That omission is a reason to verify coverage before claiming an exhaustive ranking, not to discard every correctly recorded innings. Keep the competition, gender, date, actual balls and source beside each row. IPL records and World Cup-only records belong in their own comparisons. An exceptional historical start is evidence of what happened once, not a normal powerplay expectation or a future fantasy projection.
