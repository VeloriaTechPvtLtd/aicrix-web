Dead ball means that play on that ball has ended. It does not automatically erase the delivery, remove runs or require the bowler to bowl again. A boundary is an everyday counterexample: the ball becomes dead, but an otherwise valid delivery still counts.

This guide uses the [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf) and [ICC men’s Test conditions effective October 2026](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf). Its examples use six-ball overs, not an assumed rule for every domestic competition.

## The call and the event are different

A boundary or dismissal can make the ball dead without a separate dead-ball call being necessary. An umpire may signal to inform players. Other situations require intervention, including a possibly serious injury, an accidental pre-delivery drop, or an unassisted bail falling before the striker can play. [MCC Law 20.1 and 20.4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

That distinction matters when watching a replay. The time at which a microphone picks up the words is not necessarily the legal stopping point. Start with the incident, then identify the provision governing it.

For required calls, the normal timing is the incident causing dead ball. Expressly delayed calls use the call itself instead. Once dead, the ball cannot be restored to live play for that delivery by reversing a decision. [MCC Law 20.4 and 20.6](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

## A stationary ball is not yours to declare dead

MCC’s [February 2026 explanation](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/86ac7ad2-1031-4e2b-bdea-ff407e7b0db0/Ten-major-changes-to-Laws-for-2026-edition_6.pdf) describes a broader definition of finally settled: the ball can be held by any fielder or stationary on the ground, with the umpire determining that it is no longer in play. It need not have reached the bowler or wicketkeeper. The change gives the umpire judgment; it does not give a batter a personal stop-play button.

Imagine a ball lying beside a fielder while the batters consider another run. A photograph shows its position, but not the umpire’s decision or the surrounding play. Neither “it stopped moving” nor “someone wanted to keep running” is a sufficient standalone explanation.

## Opportunity to play is the starting test

Usually, a delivered ball stopped before the striker has an opportunity to play does not count. After that opportunity, it normally counts unless a no-ball, wide or specific exception applies. Recognized distraction is one such exception. A ball never delivered cannot count as one of the over’s six. [ICC clauses 17.3 and 20.7](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

The word “opportunity” is important. A batter can leave a delivery without attempting a stroke and still have had the opportunity to play it. Do not use “no bat contact” as a substitute test.

Readiness has its own two-part protection: no attempted stroke and an adequate reason for being unready. The umpire must accept the reason; otherwise that provision does not supply a replacement ball. Distraction while preparing for or receiving the ball is addressed separately. [ICC clauses 20.4.2.5–20.4.2.6 and 20.7.2](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

## A fictional over in the scorebook

Start at 50/2 after 12.3 overs, meaning 75 valid deliveries. Assume no penalties, wickets or miscount, and that the umpires make the stated decisions.

| Event | Score afterwards | Overs afterwards | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Valid delivery hit for four | 54/2 | 12.4 | Boundary ends play but the delivery counts |
| Wind dislodges a striker’s bail before an opportunity to play; dead ball | 54/2 | 12.4 | No valid delivery added |
| Umpire recognizes distraction during receipt; dead ball, no runs | 54/2 | 12.4 | Specific non-counting exception |
| Ordinary valid dot ball, then settled | 54/2 | 12.5 | One more valid delivery |

Four events have advanced the count by only two: 75 to 77 valid balls. Yet both counted deliveries also became dead. This is why counting dead-ball signals cannot reconstruct an over, and counting score-feed rows cannot reliably reconstruct legal balls.

## Keep runs and ball count in separate columns

A no-ball call does not itself make the ball dead. Conversely, a dead-ball event is not inherently a no-ball or an extra. Check the applicable scoring provision rather than adding or removing runs merely because play stopped. [ICC clause 21.13 and Law 20 framework](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

Record three things separately: the reason play ended, the runs allowed, and whether a valid delivery was added. A compact score feed that says only “dead ball” is missing the information needed to answer the other two questions.
