A right-arm leg-spinner’s stock **leg break** turns from the leg side towards the off side of a right-handed batter after pitching. For a left-handed batter, the same spin direction brings the ball in towards the batter instead. A **googly** from that bowler turns the opposite way to the stock leg break. [Liam Sanders, 2019 doctoral thesis, section 2.2](https://drpaulfelton.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2019-sanders-factors-affecting-performance-in-elite-finger-spin-bowlers.pdf)

That is why “the ball turns away” is an incomplete description. Away from whom? A change of batter can change the answer even when the bowler uses the same delivery.

## Use the batter as the reference point

For this comparison, keep the bowler fixed as a **right-arm leg-spinner**:

| Delivery | Right-handed batter | Left-handed batter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock leg break | Leg side towards off side, away from the batter | Off side towards leg side, into the batter |
| Googly | Off side towards leg side, into the batter | Leg side towards off side, away from the batter |

These are directional descriptions, not promises about how far a particular ball will deviate. The table is specifically for a right-arm leg-spinner; do not apply it to a left-arm bowler without changing the reference.

## A bowler’s label is not a delivery log

Calling someone a leg-spinner describes a bowling style. It does not establish which variation was used for every ball. A matchup note needs to distinguish the player’s general category from the delivery actually bowled.

For example, consider a right-hander and left-hander sharing a partnership. A note saying “away turn” for the whole over may become wrong after a single changes the striker. Record the bowler’s arm, the batter’s handedness and the variation, rather than treating one phrase as a permanent property of the over.

Screen-left and screen-right are also poor substitutes for those terms: a replay from the opposite end reverses the viewer’s orientation. Leg side and off side keep the explanation attached to the batter rather than the broadcast camera.

## A real example of changing the plan

During Australia’s 2019 World Cup match against New Zealand, Steve Smith dismissed the right-handed Colin de Grandhomme with leg-spin. With left-handers Jimmy Neesham and Tom Latham then at the crease, Smith switched to off-spin. Cricket Australia’s report explains that his leg breaks would have turned into the left-handers towards a short leg-side boundary. [Cricket Australia, 4 July 2019](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/3303512/freak-smith-unveils-exciting-new-weapon)

The useful lesson is not that one style always beats one handedness. In this incident, the striker and boundary layout changed the tactical calculation. It also shows why a player’s usual bowling description cannot replace observation of the actual spell.

## What this tells you about a matchup

Direction helps explain a plan; it does not supply a wicket probability. Before turning a bowling-style label into a player comparison, ask whether you know the actual variation, the batting hand and the relevant match context.

For a beginner, the reliable starting point is simple: **identify the bowling arm, then the delivery, then the batter’s handedness**. That sequence prevents the common mistake of calling a leg break “away spin” for every batter.
