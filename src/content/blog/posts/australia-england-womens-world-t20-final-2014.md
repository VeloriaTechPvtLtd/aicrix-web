Australia beat England by six wickets in the 2014 Women’s World T20 final in Mirpur on 6 April. England made 105/8 from 20 overs; Australia reached 106/4 in 15.1 overs, winning a third consecutive title with 29 balls remaining.

Sarah Coyte’s 3/16 restricted England before Meg Lanning’s 44 from 30 balls and Ellyse Perry’s unbeaten 31 from 32 completed the chase. The result was more comfortable than [Australia’s previous final against England in 2012](/blog/australia-england-womens-world-t20-final-2012), which had been decided by four runs.

## Coyte kept the target within reach

Coyte dismissed Charlotte Edwards, Sarah Taylor and Tammy Beaumont. England finished at 105/8, leaving Australia a target of 106. Coyte received the player-of-the-match award, recognising the bowling contribution that preceded the chase.

That distinction matters when remembering a final often associated with Lanning. Australia did not need an exceptional scoring rate to win: 106 from 120 legal deliveries meant an initial requirement of 5.3 runs per over. The batting task was to preserve control while making progress, rather than recover from an imposing total.

## A 60-run partnership settled the chase

Lanning and Perry added 60 for the third wicket. Their contributions were complementary rather than identical: Lanning scored 44 from 30 balls, while Perry remained unbeaten with 31 from 32.

Lanning was dismissed with only two runs required. Perry stayed to finish the chase, and Australia reached the target after 91 legal deliveries. In cricket notation, 15.1 means 15 overs and one ball, not 15.1 decimal overs. Subtracting those 91 deliveries from a 120-ball innings gives the reported 29 balls remaining.

## What the six-wicket margin means

The six-wicket result describes Australia’s unused wickets when the chase ended. The 29 unused deliveries supply the other part of the picture: Australia had time as well as wickets available. Neither measure should be confused with the four-run winning margin from the 2012 final, when Australia batted first.

This final combined a modest target, a decisive third-wicket partnership and an early finish. Coyte’s wickets and the Lanning–Perry stand explain that sequence more precisely than treating the trophy as a batting achievement alone.
