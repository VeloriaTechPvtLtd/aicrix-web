**A declaration closes an innings that has begun; a forfeiture gives up an innings before it starts.** Neither should be confused with conceding the match. Under the 2026 MCC Laws and October 2026 ICC men’s Test conditions, neither option is available for the final innings of the match.

That last restriction matters when reading older explanations. The [ICC’s 26 September 2017 clause guide](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/mens-test-match-clause-15-declaration-and-forfeiture) describes declaration and forfeiture without the final-innings exception now present in the governing text. A page can be official and still describe an older rule.

## Which decision is available

The [MCC’s 2017 Code, fourth edition 2026](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), Law 15, distinguishes the two actions:

| Decision | Timing | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Declaration | During a permitted innings, when the ball is dead | Closes that innings with its existing score |
| Forfeiture | Before a permitted innings begins | Gives up that innings without batting in it |
| Either action in the final innings | Not permitted under the cited 2026 text | Cannot be used to close the match that way |

The captain must notify the opposing captain and the umpires. Once notified, the decision cannot be changed. Both kinds of innings count as completed; completion does not require ten dismissals.

These are the Law-based distinctions, corroborated for men’s Tests by [ICC clause 15](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf). For a domestic first-class fixture, check its competition and season conditions rather than assuming every regulation is identical. This guide does not extend the options to ODIs or T20s.

## Declaration leaves runs on the board

Imagine a fictional two-innings match with no penalty adjustments. A makes 350 in its first innings and B replies with 300. A then reaches 150/4 in its second innings and declares.

A has an aggregate of 350 + 150 = 500. B already has 300, so it needs 201 in its second innings to finish one run ahead. The 150 declaration total is not the target; the earlier fifty-run lead matters too.

The four dismissed batters do not become ten because the captain stops batting. The decision surrenders the remaining opportunity to add runs. It does not erase the 150 already scored or transfer unused wickets into B’s chase.

The match can now reach different outcomes:

| Hypothetical B second innings | Aggregate comparison | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 201/6 | B 501, A 500 | B wins by four wickets |
| 190 all out | B 490, A 500 | A wins by ten runs |
| 200 all out | B 500, A 500; all innings complete | Tie |
| 200/9 when the match ends for time | Scores level, B innings unfinished | Draw |

These examples apply the [ICC result provisions in clauses 16.1, 16.3 and 16.5](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf), assuming no concession, award or exceptional scoring adjustment. Equal aggregate scores alone do not establish a tie.

## Forfeiture removes an unstarted opportunity

Now reset to the same fictional first innings: A 350 and B 300. Instead of starting its second innings, A forfeits it. Assume that innings adds no penalty runs.

A’s aggregate remains 350, so B needs 51 in its second innings to win. There is no 150 to include because A never made the second-innings score from the earlier example. This is a third-innings forfeiture, not a forbidden forfeiture of the match’s final innings.

The visible batting sequence would be A’s first innings, B’s first innings, then B’s second innings, with A’s forfeited innings recorded between them. That does not mean B followed on. [ICC clause 13.2](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf) treats follow-on and forfeiture as distinct exceptions to alternating innings.

The arithmetic shows what the choice changes; it does not recommend it. Forgoing a chance to increase a lead can make the opposition’s task smaller, and no number here establishes the likelihood of either side winning.

## An innings forfeit is not a match concession

The word “forfeit” can be misleading without its object. Giving up an innings under Law 15 is different from conceding defeat or refusing to play under the result provisions. The latter can decide the match; the former changes which batting opportunities remain. [MCC Laws 15 and 16](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

A declaration also ends an innings, not necessarily the day. Intervals and close-of-play rules decide when the next innings can begin. Nor does a declaration itself enforce the follow-on: that is a separate decision with separate eligibility conditions.

To read a scorecard correctly, identify which innings was closed, whether it had started, the runs already scored and the batting opportunities still available. Those facts explain the target more reliably than treating every early innings ending as the same event.
