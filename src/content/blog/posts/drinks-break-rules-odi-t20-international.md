Scheduled drinks, a quick drink for one player and the interval between innings are different events. Seeing someone carry water onto the field does not by itself mean a full drinks break has started. The format and applicable playing conditions decide which permissions apply.

This guide uses October 2026 international conditions. The [ICC announcement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/key-changes-across-formats-with-new-icc-playing-conditions) makes the package applicable to series starting on or after 1 October, while ongoing series retain the previous version. Domestic competitions may have their own arrangements.

## The scheduled break depends on the format

Under [men’s ODI clause 11.5](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf), two drinks breaks per session are permitted, spaced 70 minutes apart. Umpires may allow extra intervals in extreme heat. That is a time-based provision, not a universal instruction to stop after a particular over.

For [men’s T20Is](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf) and [women’s T20Is](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/o3vhm0zw7pppoxpgopke.pdf), clause 11.5 permits a 2-minute-30-second midpoint interval in each innings if both participating boards agreed beforehand. In interrupted matches scheduled for 14 overs or fewer, there is no drinks break. After a lengthy delay, the referee may cancel remaining breaks.

## Two shortened T20I examples

These hypothetical cases use the women’s T20I provision, not a domestic league rule. Assume rain has reduced both innings before play and the boards previously agreed to drinks.

| Revised allocation | What the comparison establishes |
|---|---|
| 14 overs per side | The no-drinks exception applies. A seven-over midpoint does not restore the break. |
| 16 overs per side | The allocation alone does not remove drinks. The midpoint is eight overs, but the referee’s cancellation discretion still matters. |

Fourteen and sixteen are only two overs apart, yet they fall on different sides of an explicit threshold. Calculating the halfway point is therefore not enough: first check whether the scheduled break exists. These examples do not reconstruct a mid-innings rain interruption or prescribe a revised finishing time.

## A drink for one player is not a team interval

Men’s ODI clause 11.5.2 permits an individual drink at the boundary edge, or on the field when a wicket falls, without wasting playing time. Other drinks brought onto the field need umpire permission. The T20I documents contain the same individual-drink provision.

That distinction helps explain why play can continue around a player receiving water. Do not interpret the absence of a scheduled break as a ban on every drink, or the presence of a bottle as permission to stop the whole match.

## Coaches and practice are separate questions

The [ICC’s October explanation](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/key-changes-across-formats-with-new-icc-playing-conditions) describes ODI/T20I alignment allowing the head coach or designate to enter for tactical consultation at drinks. This is permission for the specified interval, not unrestricted access during play. The T20I drinks clause prohibits practice during the break.

## Do not confuse drinks with changing innings

Men’s T20I clause 11.2.1 sets a 15-minute innings interval. Following a lengthy delay before the first innings finishes, clause 11.4.2 permits the referee to reduce it, but not below ten minutes. That is a separate clock from the 2½-minute drinks interval.

To interpret a particular stoppage, identify the competition, rule version, type of interval and latest revised allocation. Then check any official cancellation or rescheduling. A commentary label such as “drinks” is useful shorthand, but it is not enough to explain every pause or coaching visit.
