A delivery bouncing twice is not automatically judged by where the batter happens to stand. The second bounce’s position relative to the popping crease matters. A ball landing off the pitch has a different cutoff: the line of the striker’s wicket. A ball that stops short introduces a further distinction between no-ball and dead ball.

This guide uses the [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf) and [ICC men’s Test conditions effective October 2026](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf). Its examples isolate these delivery-path rules, not every possible reason for a no-ball. Assume the umpire regards the ball as delivered, with no prior contact with the striker’s bat or person and no other infringement unless stated.

## Second bounce before which line

MCC Law 21.7.1 requires no-ball when the delivery bounces more than once, or rolls along the ground, before reaching the striker’s popping crease. The test is not “before reaching the batter” or “before reaching the stumps.” [MCC Law 21.7](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Consider two fictional deliveries whose first bounce is well before that crease. In A, the second bounce is also clearly before it. In B, the second bounce is clearly beyond it, between the popping crease and wicket. A triggers this no-ball rule. B does not trigger it merely because it has bounced twice.

B is not certified legal in every respect. A front-foot infringement or another applicable rule could still matter. The narrower conclusion is that the second-bounce location alone does not supply this particular no-ball. That distinction prevents a correct answer about one test becoming an unsupported answer about the whole delivery.

## Off pitch uses the wicket line

MCC Law 21.7.2 covers a first landing or subsequent bounce wholly or partly off the defined pitch before the ball reaches the line of the striker’s wicket. Its non-turf provision refers to the artificial surface. This is a different spatial test from counting bounces before the popping crease. [MCC Law 21.7](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Now modify fictional delivery B. Its second bounce is still beyond the popping crease but before the wicket line. This time the bounce is partly off the pitch. The second-bounce timing does not trigger 21.7.1, but the off-pitch landing can trigger 21.7.2. One delivery can pass one test and fail another.

Do not use the edge of a television camera’s crop as the pitch boundary. A reliable description needs the actual playing area, the relevant line and the bounce location. If those cannot be established, a still image cannot resolve the question.

## A no ball does not always stop play

ICC clause 21.13 says the no-ball call does not itself make the ball dead. Clause 21.8, however, specifically requires no-ball followed immediately by dead ball when an untouched delivery stops before the striker’s wicket line, or is so far from the pitch that playing it would require the striker to leave the pitch. [ICC clauses 21.8 and 21.13](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

| Fictional delivery path | Relevant distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Second bounce before the popping crease; ball continues normally | No-ball under the bounce rule, not automatically dead from that call alone |
| Second bounce beyond the popping crease, on the pitch, before the wicket | Not a no-ball solely for that second bounce |
| Bounce partly off the pitch before the wicket line | Off-pitch no-ball test applies |
| Ball comes to rest short of the striker’s wicket without prior bat or body contact | No-ball and immediate dead ball under 21.8 |

Imagine the final row’s ball resting two metres short of the wicket line. The batter walks forward and hits it towards the boundary after it has become dead. That later hit cannot revive the delivery or create a boundary from it. Contrast the first row, where assuming that every no-ball has already stopped play would be the error.

## Can a second bounce still lead to bowled

For the otherwise lawful on-pitch version of B, a second bounce beyond the popping crease does not itself prevent a bowled dismissal. The ordinary bowled conditions still apply. Conversely, a no-ball cannot dismiss the striker bowled. [ICC clauses 32.1 and 21.17](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

These examples do not adjudicate a historical clip. Old matches require their contemporary conditions, and a description saying only “double bounce” omits the location that decides the current test. Nor does this guide infer a free hit from the general Laws; that belongs to the applicable competition rules.

Use two location labels in your notes: popping crease for the multiple-bounce test, wicket line for off-pitch landing or stopping short. Then record whether the controlling provision stops play immediately. That sequence answers the delivery-path question without confusing it with a full scorebook or free-hit calculation.
