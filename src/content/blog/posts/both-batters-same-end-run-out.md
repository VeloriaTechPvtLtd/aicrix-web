Two batters can finish at the same end without either being run out at that instant. The missing detail is which wicket the fielders break. Before identifying a dismissed batter, assign a ground to each batter; being physically beside a partner does not give both batters ownership of that end.

This guide uses ordinary running situations without a runner. The relevant provisions are Law 30.2 and Law 38.4 in the MCC 2026 edition, with matching ground-allocation provisions in the ICC men’s Test conditions effective October 2026. The fictional examples assume a live ball, a fair wicket break by the fielding side, an appeal and no run-out exception.

## Assign the grounds before naming the batter

Under [MCC Law 30.2](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), a batter already alone in a ground retains it when the partner joins. If one subsequently leaves, the remaining batter owns that ground. The opposite ground belongs to the other batter. If neither ground contains a batter, proximity decides; at an exact level, use the positions immediately before they drew level.

Then apply [ICC clause 38.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf): the relevant batter is the one whose ground is at the broken wicket. This is an identification step within the run-out conditions, not a rule that every broken wicket produces a dismissal.

## One mix-up with two different outcomes

Imagine two ends labelled South and North, and batters A and B. A is grounded at South before B arrives there. Both remain at South. These are alternative endings to that same starting situation, not two wickets in one play.

| Wicket fairly broken | Ground assignment | Outcome under the stated assumptions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| South | South belongs to A; North to B | Neither is run out: A is in A’s ground, and B’s wicket has not been broken |
| North | South belongs to A; North to B | B is run out, despite standing beside A at South |

The important distinction is between a person’s location and the ground allocated to that person. A photograph showing two bats behind the South crease does not establish that both batters are safe from a throw to North. Conversely, breaking South is not enough merely because the scene looks like an obvious mix-up.

## The first arrival does not keep that ground forever

Continue from the same setup, but change what happens before the wicket is broken. A now leaves South while B stays grounded there. South belongs to B and North to A. If the fielders then fairly break North while A remains outside it, A is the batter dismissed.

That change explains why a replay needs a sequence rather than a single frame. Record who occupied South first, whether both were there together, and whether one left before the wicket-breaking instant. The original arrival order alone gives the wrong answer after this departure.

## What if neither batter reaches a crease

If A is nearer South and B nearer North while neither is in a ground, those are their respective grounds. At the instant they draw level, retain the assignment from immediately before that instant. Exact equality is not a licence to choose whichever batter seems more responsible for the confusion.

For a simple replay worksheet, use three columns: time, South owner and North owner. Add a row when the relevant positions change, then mark the wicket-breaking instant. Keep descriptions such as “called yes” or “turned back late” in a separate notes column. They may explain the mistake but are not substitutes for the ownership test.

## Keep the other decisions separate

The ground-allocation question does not settle whether the wicket was broken fairly, whether the ball was still live or how many runs count. Nor does it determine who should face the next delivery. Solve those questions using their own rules rather than making the answer to one stand in for all the others.

Start with the sequence of positions, identify the broken end, and only then identify the batter. That method is more reliable than either shortcut: “both are safe because both are behind a crease” or “the second arrival is always out.”
