**Pakistan beat South Africa by 29 runs under the Duckworth/Lewis method at Eden Park, Auckland, on 7 March 2015.** Pakistan made 222 all out, but South Africa’s revised target was 232 from 47 overs. South Africa were dismissed for 202 in 33.3 overs. [Contemporary match report](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2015-03-07/pakistan-shocks-south-africa-cricket-world-cup/6288448).

The difference between 222 and 202 is only 20. It is not the winning margin because the chase was governed by the revised target. This Pool B match also featured a late Pakistan collapse, three three-wicket spells and six catches by wicketkeeper Sarfaraz Ahmed.

The revised target required 232 to win, with 231 the tie-equivalent total. Subtracting South Africa’s 202 from 231 gives the official 29-run margin. The 30 runs still needed to win and the 29-run defeat describe different quantities.

Pakistan’s actual batting total remained 222. The adjustment did not add runs to any batter’s score, and it cannot be reproduced by simply scaling 222 from one over allocation to another. The published historical target is the governing value for this match.

## Pakistan lost its last five wickets for ten runs

Misbah-ul-Haq top-scored with 56 from 86 balls. Sarfaraz contributed 49 before being run out, while Younis Khan made 37. Dale Steyn finished with 3/30. Pakistan reached 212 with five wickets down before the remaining wickets fell for only ten additional runs. [Innings record](https://www.cricbuzz.com/live-cricket-scorecard/12883/pak-vs-rsa-29th-match-pool-b-icc-cricket-world-cup-2015).

The ICC’s account identifies Afridi’s dismissal as the start of that final collapse. Sarfaraz had earlier combined attacking strokes with a costly attempt at a second run, ending one short of fifty. His contribution to the result would extend well beyond that innings. [ICC key moments](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/south-africa-v-pakistan-6-key-moments).

Pakistan used 280 legal balls, two fewer than its revised 282-ball allocation. The small number of unused deliveries matters: this was not a side leaving ten overs untouched through a premature all-out. Its problem was the cluster of wickets near the finish.

## The chase ran out of wickets rather than time

South Africa recovered from losing Quinton de Kock on the second ball to reach 67 before losing Faf du Plessis and Hashim Amla at the same team score. The next two wickets left them 77/5. AB de Villiers then kept the chase alive with 77 from 58 balls. [South Africa innings ledger](https://www.mykhel.com/cricket/south-africa-vs-pakistan-scorecard-world-cup-s9985-560882/).

When de Villiers was caught behind off Sohail Khan at 32.2 overs, the score was 200/9. South Africa still had 88 legal balls available but needed 32 runs with only one wicket standing. That was an asking rate of just 2.18 per over, yet the batting resources were almost exhausted.

Only two further runs were added before Imran Tahir was caught behind seven legal balls later. South Africa finished with 81 deliveries unused. A low required rate did not make the chase safe: the final pair still had to survive long enough to score those runs.

Sarfaraz took catches to dismiss de Kock, du Plessis, Amla, Steyn, de Villiers and Tahir. The ICC’s contemporary report records his six catches and Player of the Match award. These were wicketkeeping catches, not six outfield catches or six additional wickets on top of the bowlers’ ten. [ICC match account](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/south-africa-v-pakistan-6-key-moments).

The scorecard therefore tells a fuller story than a modest first-innings total alone: Pakistan’s late collapse left a rain-adjusted chase, South Africa lost its top order, and de Villiers’ counterattack ended before the target was reached. The historical result offers no guarantee about another meeting between these teams.
