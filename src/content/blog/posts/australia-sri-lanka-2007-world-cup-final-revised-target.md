Australia won the 2007 men's ODI World Cup final by 53 runs under the Duckworth–Lewis method. The match also ended in controversy: the ICC subsequently acknowledged that officials had wrongly required play to continue after a bad-light stoppage. The batting performance, the adjusted result and the procedural mistake are three separate parts of the story. [ICC's statement on the final](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/icc-statement-regarding-appointment-of-officials-for-the-icc-world-twenty20-in-south-africa)

Keeping those parts separate resolves two common questions. Why was the margin not simply the difference between the teams' totals? And why did the players return when the contest already appeared to have reached its conclusion?

## Gilchrist changed the scale of the innings

Adam Gilchrist scored 149 from 104 balls as Australia reached 281 for four in a rain-reduced allocation of 38 overs. Sri Lanka's eventual revised requirement was 269 from 36 overs, and they finished on 215 for eight. Australia secured their third consecutive men's World Cup. [ICC's 2007 overview](https://www.icc-cricket.com/tournaments/cricketworldcup/news/mens-cricket-world-cup-2007-overview)

Gilchrist's share of Australia's total was approximately 53.0 per cent, calculated as 149 divided by 281. That is a share of team runs, including the team's extras in the denominator. It is not the percentage of deliveries he faced or the proportion of the partnership he supplied.

His 149 from 104 balls gives an innings strike rate of 143.27 runs per 100 balls. This is calculated as 149 ÷ 104 × 100.

## The second-wicket stand gave Sri Lanka a response

Sanath Jayasuriya and Kumar Sangakkara added 116 for Sri Lanka's second wicket. The ICC's historical finals review records Jayasuriya's 63 and Sangakkara's 54, alongside the innings totals and the official 53-run Duckworth–Lewis result. [ICC's historical finals review](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/six-months-to-cwc-2011-final)

The records should also preserve the historical method's name. This final is recorded as a Duckworth–Lewis result. Replacing that label casually with today's familiar DLS terminology obscures which method was applied at the time.

## Why 281 minus 215 is not the winning margin

Subtracting the raw team totals produces 66, not the official margin of 53. The reason is visible in the unequal innings allocations and revised target. Sri Lanka were not completing the same 38-over assignment against an unchanged target.

With a winning target of 269, the corresponding level-score threshold is 268. Subtracting Sri Lanka's 215 from 268 gives the recorded 53-run margin. Sri Lanka needed 54 additional runs to reach the winning target. That distinction between a target and the level-score threshold explains the apparent one-run difference.

This checks the published margin, not the underlying resource-table calculation that produced the target of 269.

## What went wrong at the end

The ICC said the officials mistakenly believed the match would need completion the following day after bad light, although the minimum 20 overs in Sri Lanka's innings had already been played. The final three overs were consequently completed in near darkness. The ICC subsequently withheld appointments for those officials at the ensuing World Twenty20. [ICC's statement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/icc-statement-regarding-appointment-of-officials-for-the-icc-world-twenty20-in-south-africa)

That finding concerns the procedure used to finish this particular match. It is not a general instruction that every modern interrupted ODI ends once 20 overs have been bowled. Contemporary playing conditions and the actual interruption must be checked for a current fixture.

The final remains both a major batting achievement and a cautionary case in reading results. Gilchrist's innings explains Australia's formidable score. The revised target explains the margin. The official investigation explains the awkward closing scenes. None needs to replace the others.
