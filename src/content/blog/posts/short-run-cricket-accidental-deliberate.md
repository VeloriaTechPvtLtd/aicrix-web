A short run is not automatically a five-run offence. An accidental failure to reach the ground, a deliberate attempt to claim an uncompleted run and an honestly abandoned attempt are different situations. Identify which occurred before changing the score.

This guide uses the MCC’s 2026 Laws, with the October 2026 men’s ODI conditions as an international cross-check. A specific tournament or old match still needs its own applicable version.

## An accidental short run removes that run

In turning for another run, a batter must make good their ground. If they fall short unintentionally, that run is not scored. The next run can still count if completed, even though the earlier short turn reduced its physical length. [MCC Law 18.3–18.4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

For example, imagine A and B set off for two. A turns early at the far end, but both then complete the return. With no boundary, dismissal or other scoring event, one run counts, not two. This is not automatically a penalty of five to the opponents.

Now change one detail: both A and B were short on that same outward run. It remains one short run, not two deductions. Count the affected run, rather than multiplying the deduction by the number of batters who missed their ground. The [current ODI conditions, clause 18.4.3](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf), expressly preserve that distinction.

## Deliberate short running is a separate ruling

Under Law 18.5, the relevant deception is an attempt to appear to run more than one while deliberately failing to make good ground at an end. The umpire determines whether the action was deliberate; a viewer cannot settle intent from the score changing alone.

The batting runs are disallowed, five penalty runs are awarded to the fielding side, and its captain chooses who faces next. There is no first-offence warning stage in this provision. A no-ball or wide penalty still stands where applicable, so “every possible extra disappears” would be wrong. [MCC Laws 18.2 and 18.5](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Consider a separate invented delivery: the batting side starts at 100/3, with no extra or boundary involved. The batters pretend to complete two but deliberately cut one short. On that ruling, their score remains 100/3 and the opposition receives five penalty runs. It is neither 101/3 nor 95/3. The five are an award to the other side, not a subtraction from the existing batting total.

## Who chooses the next striker

The fielding captain’s choice is an additional consequence, not a way for the batting pair to preserve their preferred striker. The [ICC announced this change in June 2025](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/several-changes-made-to-icc-playing-conditions), before its inclusion in the 2026 MCC edition. Older explanations may therefore miss it.

If A and B were trying to ensure A faced again, a deliberate-short-run ruling does not guarantee that outcome. Treat scoring and next-striker allocation as separate decisions.

## Turning back is not necessarily deception

The [MCC’s explanation of the 2026 changes](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/86ac7ad2-1031-4e2b-bdea-ff407e7b0db0/Ten-major-changes-to-Laws-for-2026-edition_6.pdf) specifically protects an abandoned attempt when the umpires find no intention to deceive. The batters may cross and then return to their starting ends without thereby committing deliberate short running.

Suppose A and B initially judge a gap large enough to consider two, then abandon the attempt and both retreat to their original ends. Neither claims a completed run. Their change of mind is different from representing a shortened outward-and-back movement as two completed runs. Crossing by itself does not prove the offence.

## Check for a boundary before deducting

Under the [ODI conditions, clause 18.4.2](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf), an ordinary boundary means accidental short running is disregarded. An overthrow or wilful-act boundary is an exception requiring its separate calculation.

Keep a simple audit trail: attempted movement, actual completion, umpire’s classification, boundary or extra, and final award. That sequence is more reliable than a universal “runs attempted minus one” calculator. These examples isolate the short-run issue; they do not decide whether a batter was run out or a boundary was an overthrow.
