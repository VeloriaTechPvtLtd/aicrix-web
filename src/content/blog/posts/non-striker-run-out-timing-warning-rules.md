A bowler does not have to warn the non-striker before attempting a lawful run-out. But the permission is not unlimited: leaving early does not let the bowler finish the delivery action and then turn back for a dismissal under this particular rule.

The useful questions are when the wicket was broken, whether the non-striker was safely grounded, and whether the bowler subsequently delivered the ball. Those observations are more informative than simply calling an incident a “Mankad.”

## The arm, not the front foot, sets the timing limit

Under [MCC’s explanation of the timing rule](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-clarifies-law-on-non-strikers-leaving-their-gr), the relevant cutoff is the highest point of the bowler’s arm in the normal delivery swing. Once that point has been reached, this route to a non-striker run-out has closed, even if the batter had left earlier.

The [MCC FAQ](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/729142da-db9a-48eb-a5c3-16e5fdb3c42d/Law-on-non-striker-leaving-their-ground_2023.pdf) specifically distinguishes that arm movement from where the bowler’s feet land. A replay showing only the front foot cannot settle this timing question. Nor should a viewer replace the normal-action test with the moment a bowler eventually chooses to let go of the ball.

Imagine two otherwise identical attempts. In A, the bowler fairly breaks the wicket before reaching the cutoff while the non-striker remains out of their ground. In B, the bowler passes that point, stops and then breaks the wicket. The early departure is common to both; the later action is not. B cannot be treated as A simply because the batter was initially outside the crease.

## Why warnings are optional, not a first offence allowance

The [MCC World Cricket committee’s February 2023 statement](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-world-cricket-committee-calls-for-calm-on-non) confirms that a warning is not required. A player’s preference to warn an opponent is different from a prerequisite for dismissal. An otherwise valid first attempt does not become invalid merely because no previous warning was issued.

MCC’s [FAQ](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/729142da-db9a-48eb-a5c3-16e5fdb3c42d/Law-on-non-striker-leaving-their-ground_2023.pdf) acknowledges that some places developed a warning convention, but not a universal one. That distinction helps explain disagreements: a viewer may be describing the custom they grew up with while another is describing the Laws.

Keep those claims separate when discussing a match. “I would have warned the batter” expresses a sporting preference. “The umpire must reject the appeal because there was no warning” claims a rule that is not present. Neither a crowd reaction nor a commentator’s preference changes the timing evidence.

## A failed attempt is not automatically a no-ball

[Current Laws 38.3.2–38.3.3 and 21.6](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf) distinguish an undelivered ball from a delivered one. If no delivery follows, an appeal is decided; with no appeal or a not-out decision, the umpire signals dead ball promptly. It does not count in the over.

If a delivery follows and there is an appeal, a not-out decision leaves the ball in play and brings Law 21.6 into consideration. That law addresses the bowler breaking the wicket during the specified delivery period. If the non-striker is dismissed instead, the delivery does not count in the over.

Consider a simple unsuccessful attempt: the non-striker gets back before the wicket is broken, the bowler does not bowl, and the umpire gives not out. The key missing fact in an automatic “no-ball” claim is a delivery. Do not combine that example with one where the bowler carries on and bowls: the applicable branch is different.

## Practical protection is not the same as the legal cutoff

The [MCC committee](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-world-cricket-committee-calls-for-calm-on-non) recommends that non-strikers watch the ball and remain grounded until they see its release. That is a practical way to avoid this dismissal, not a replacement definition for the arm-height cutoff.

A coach can therefore give a simple instruction to wait for visible release while an umpire applies the more precise timing rule. Mixing those two purposes is how a sensible precaution becomes an inaccurate explanation.

## How to explain a replay

Watch the sequence rather than selecting the most dramatic still. Establish the normal arm movement, the instant of the wicket break, the non-striker’s grounding, and what the bowler did next. If the clip omits a necessary moment, say what is missing instead of declaring certainty.

Fairly breaking the wicket is a separate requirement from the timing test. For a particular competition, check its applicable playing conditions as well. This guide does not decide an incident from an incomplete clip.
