When an umpire points towards the middle of the pitch, the next question is not always “How many penalty runs?” A warning to a bowler following through and a warning to a batting side do not have the same sequence of consequences. To understand the decision, identify who was involved, what happened and which warning already applied.

This guide uses the MCC's October 2026 Laws as its baseline. A particular competition may have its own playing conditions, so a league-specific dispute needs the relevant competition document too.

## Find the area before judging the incident

The protected area is an imaginary central rectangle. Its end boundaries sit five feet, or 1.52 metres, in front of each popping crease. Its side boundaries are one foot, or 30.48 centimetres, either side of the line between the middle stumps. That makes the strip two feet wide, not the entire width of the pitch. [MCC Law 41.11](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

For a viewer, this is a reason to be cautious about a tight television crop. “On the pitch” and “inside this rectangle” are different descriptions. If the image does not show the crease and the player's route clearly, the location may be less obvious than the commentary makes it sound.

## A bowler's follow-through has its own sequence

Under Law 41.13, entering the area without reasonable cause during the follow-through is unfair, even without a delivery. The same bowler receives a caution, then a final warning, then suspension from bowling for the rest of that innings on a third breach. If another bowler must finish the over, they cannot have bowled in the previous over or bowl in the next. These stages apply within the innings. [MCC Law 41.13](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

The important viewing habit is to keep the player's identity attached to the warning. A highlight containing only the latest incident may omit the earlier steps. Before calling a response excessive or surprisingly lenient, establish which stage the official is dealing with.

## The batting example: Rajkot in 2024

The ICC's report from 16 February 2024 provides a concrete illustration. Ravichandran Ashwin ran down the pitch while attempting a single and was sent back by Dhruv Jurel. Joel Wilson signalled five penalty runs. India had already received the batting side's first and final warning after a Ravindra Jadeja incident on the previous day. England consequently began their innings with five runs before facing a ball. [ICC's Rajkot report](https://www.icc-cricket.com/tournaments/world-test-championship/news/india-hit-with-a-five-run-penalty-in-the-rajkot-test)

That account should not be retold as “Ashwin had already received his own warning.” The earlier incident involved a different batter. Keeping that detail prevents the apparent contradiction of a player being penalised on an incident that viewers believe was their first.

The current MCC batting provision likewise makes the warning apply throughout the innings and requires incoming batters to be informed. A subsequent breach by any batter can bring five penalty runs to the fielding side. [MCC Law 41.14](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

## A better way to follow the discussion

Use a short incident log rather than a generic count of “pitch warnings”:

- **Role:** was this the bowler's follow-through or a batter's action?

- **Person and side:** who was involved, and to whom did the earlier warning apply?

- **Timing:** was the earlier incident in this innings?

- **Official explanation:** what did the umpire actually communicate?

- **Evidence gap:** is the full sequence available, or only the final clip?

This is a reading framework, not a replacement for the umpire's judgment. It also avoids guessing intent. A report that records a decision does not automatically prove every tactical motive suggested on social media.

## Keep the score and the explanation together

The Rajkot example is especially useful because an apparently strange starting score had an explanation outside the batting innings itself. When a score looks unusual, check the extras and incident record before assuming a data error.

The lasting lesson is to follow the relevant warning sequence, not merely count footsteps. Location, role and prior warnings all matter to understanding the official decision.
