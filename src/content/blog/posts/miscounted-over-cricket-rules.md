Seven deliveries in an over do not automatically mean someone has made a mistake. A wide or no-ball can require an additional delivery without adding to the six valid balls. A genuinely miscounted over is a different issue: the umpire has counted the valid balls incorrectly.

That distinction is the first thing to establish before sharing a screenshot or declaring that a wicket should disappear from the scorecard. Count the events, then identify which events count towards the over.

## Six valid balls, not necessarily six deliveries

MCC Law 17 sets the normal over at six valid balls. Wides and no-balls do not count towards those six, and the Law specifies other excluded deliveries. It also says that, if an umpire miscounts, the over as counted by that umpire stands. The rule recognises the counting error without pretending that the play which followed never happened. [MCC 2026 Laws, 17.1–17.5](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Here is a fictional comparison. In one sequence, a bowler delivers six valid balls and one wide. There are seven deliveries but no miscount. In another, the bowler delivers seven valid balls because the over was allowed to continue. The number of deliveries is the same, but the explanation is not.

This is why a seven-entry commentary list is insufficient evidence by itself. Each entry needs to be read, not merely counted.

## The 2026 wording change matters

MCC's explanation of its October 2026 amendments shows a change in Law 17.5.2 from discretionary to mandatory wording. After a miscount allows play to continue beyond six valid balls, the umpire is to call Over when the ball becomes dead after a subsequent delivery, even if that delivery is not valid. Older online reproductions may still carry the previous wording. [MCC's 2026 changes document](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/5ff72819-c9ef-448c-87e3-b051408e1803/Changes-to-Laws-for-2026-edition-explained_2.pdf)

For a reader comparing sources, the date is part of the answer. A search result can be from an official organisation and still describe an older edition. That does not make the old document fraudulent; it makes it the wrong document to cite as newly current without qualification.

This guide uses the MCC baseline. For a particular professional competition, check the playing conditions governing that match rather than assuming every archived regulation is interchangeable.

## A wicket on the extra ball: the Klinger example

Cricket Australia's report dated 14 January 2019 describes Michael Klinger being caught on the seventh ball of an over during Perth Scorchers' match against Sydney Sixers. A Cricket Australia spokesperson acknowledged an apparent miscount and said the decision stood because the ball had been bowled and was live. Perth nevertheless won by seven wickets. [Cricket Australia's report](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/3253025)

This is a historical example, not an application of the 2026 amendment to an earlier match. Its value is that it separates three facts which are often muddled: an error occurred, the dismissal remained in the match record, and the affected team still won.

It does not tell us what Klinger would have scored had the over ended earlier. Any claim about that alternative innings would be speculation. A clear explanation can acknowledge the player's disadvantage without inventing the match that might have happened.

## A practical check before calling an over wrong

Start with the delivery record and write a second count containing only valid balls. Mark wides and no-balls separately. If another interruption occurred, establish its treatment from the relevant rule rather than guessing from a commentary abbreviation.

Next, locate the official explanation, if one exists. Keep your own reconstruction distinct from a confirmed admission of miscount. If the record is incomplete, leave the conclusion open.

Finally, preserve the recorded outcome. An analysis can describe why a decision was mistaken without silently editing away runs or wickets. That is particularly important when comparing a highlight clip with a scorecard.

## The takeaway

An over normally contains six valid balls, but delivery count alone does not prove a counting error. When a genuine miscount occurs, distinguish the mistake from the legal treatment of subsequent play. Read the sequence, use the correct rules edition and avoid rewriting the match to fit a hypothetical outcome.
