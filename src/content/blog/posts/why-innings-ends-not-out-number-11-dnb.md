The eleventh player **can bat**. In an ordinary eleven-player innings, two batters begin and nine others can join them. After nine wickets, the final pair can still score. Once a tenth batter is dismissed, the survivor has no partner and cannot continue alone. “All out” therefore need not mean that every player was dismissed. [MCC Laws, Law 13.3](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

The player left not out is not necessarily No. 11. It could be an opener who survived while wickets fell at the other end. Batting position tells you when someone entered; dismissal status tells you how their innings finished.

## No 11 can make a major contribution

James Anderson demonstrated the distinction against India at Trent Bridge in July 2014. Batting at No. 11, he scored 81 before being dismissed. Joe Root finished unbeaten on 154 after the pair added 198 for the last wicket. The last player to enter was the last player dismissed, while his partner was left not out. [ICC match report, 12 July 2014](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/england-ahead-after-root-anderson-heroics)

That example answers two different questions. No. 11 is a batting position, not an instruction to remain in the dressing room. And a not-out score does not guarantee the player was batting last in the order.

## The overs can finish before the wickets do

A limited-overs innings can end because its allocated overs are complete. Men’s T20Is normally allow up to 20 overs per side; losing all ten wickets is not required. [ICC men’s T20I conditions, clauses 13.3 and 13.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf)

For a simple example, imagine a side finishes its 20 overs on 150/6. Assume a normal eleven-player team, with no retirements or unavailable batters. Six players have been dismissed, two are at the crease, and three have not entered. The total is complete even though the team has four wickets left.

Those three unused batters have not failed to obey a requirement to bat. Their turn simply was not needed before the innings ended.

## A successful chase stops the batting too

In a normal chase, reaching the winning target ends the match. The remaining batters do not then take turns merely to complete the scorecard. MCC Law 13 also provides for other forms of innings completion, including declarations where allowed, or a wicket or retirement leaving no further batter available. [MCC Laws, Laws 13.3 and 16.6](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

Suppose a team needs 101 and reaches 101/1. With no retirements, only three players have batted: the two openers and the replacement for the dismissed opener. Eight can finish with DNB beside their names despite the team winning comfortably.

## Not out and DNB describe different situations

**DNB** means did not bat. It is not the same as entering the innings and finishing **not out**, and it does not by itself mean the player was absent from the playing XI.

Facing a delivery is not the sole test of whether an innings has begun. Under MCC Law 25.2, an incoming batter’s innings ordinarily starts on entering the field; the opening pair and new batters after a resumption start at the call of Play. [MCC Laws, Law 25.2](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

A player can therefore enter, never face a ball, and finish on 0 not out when the partner completes the chase. That is different from never entering at all. Read the scorecard’s status as well as its runs and balls: a zero in one column cannot explain the whole innings.
