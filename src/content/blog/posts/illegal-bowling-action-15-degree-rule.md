A bowler being reported for a suspect action is not the same as a bowler being found to have an illegal action. That distinction is essential when reading cricket news. One announcement starts a review; another records what the assessment found. Treating the first as the second can turn a procedural update into an unfair accusation.

The other common confusion concerns the number itself. The ICC's 15-degree tolerance concerns elbow extension during a defined part of the delivery, not a photograph showing how bent a player's arm happens to look.

## Extension is movement, not a frozen pose

The ICC describes an illegal action as elbow extension exceeding 15 degrees between the arm reaching horizontal and ball release. Its regulations define extension as movement from a flexed elbow towards a straighter position. Hyperextension, movement beyond the straight position, is discounted in determining legality. These definitions concern the ICC assessment framework, not a diagnosis that a viewer can make from appearance alone. [ICC regulations linked from its current explainer](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/ogrrdz3olvinuhtdia2d.pdf)

Consider a simplified, fictional illustration. If an arm looks bent in a still image, that image does not tell you how much its angle changed during the relevant interval. Likewise, drawing two lines on a television screenshot does not recreate a formal assessment. The image might help someone ask a question, but it cannot supply all the evidence needed to answer it.

A useful phrase is “change in elbow angle.” It keeps attention on movement rather than inviting a judgment about a player's anatomy.

## Why an official report is not the final verdict

The ICC explains that match officials use their observation and experience to identify a suspected problem. Testing then takes place at an accredited centre under specialists in human movement. If the action is found illegal, the player is suspended from bowling until modification and a successful further test. This separates the act of raising a concern from the process of determining whether that concern is confirmed. [ICC illegal-actions explainer](https://www.icc-cricket.com/about/cricket/rules-and-regulations/illegal-bowling-actions?appview=true)

For a fan following a case, keep a short chronology rather than relying on the first headline you remember. Record the reporting announcement, the assessment and the subsequent official decision. Do not silently fill a gap between those stages with a guessed outcome.

## A documented example: Matthew Kuhnemann in 2025

On 12 February 2025, the ICC announced that Matthew Kuhnemann had been reported following the second Test against Sri Lanka in Galle. The release described concerns and an upcoming independent assessment; it did not announce an adverse finding. [ICC reporting announcement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/media-releases/kuhnemann-reported-for-suspect-bowling-action)

On 26 February, the ICC confirmed that his action had been found legal. Its statement said the assessment took place in Brisbane on 15 February and that all his deliveries were within the permitted tolerance. That later announcement is indispensable to an accurate account of this historical episode. [ICC assessment outcome](https://www.icc-cricket.com/media-releases/bowling-action-of-kuhnemann-found-to-be-legal)

This example illustrates why an old report cannot be recycled as proof of a current problem. It also shows why “reported,” “tested” and “found illegal” should never be used as interchangeable labels.

## A checklist for reading the next headline

**Check the date.** Is the article a new decision or an old report resurfacing during a different match?

**Identify the stage.** Does the source describe suspicion, an assessment result or a later reassessment?

**Read the exact scope.** Avoid expanding a statement about bowling into an unsupported claim about every form of participation.

**Find the governing source.** A clip, opinion post or commentary remark is not a substitute for an official finding.

**Keep uncertainty visible.** If you have found the report but not a subsequent result, say that the outcome has not been verified. Do not convert missing information into guilt.

## What this guide does not establish

This is an explanation of terminology and evidence, not an assessment of any reader's action. It does not prescribe rehabilitation, certify a bowling technique or claim that international procedures apply unchanged to every local competition. Players and clubs should use the process specified by their own governing competition.

The practical takeaway is simple: distinguish the movement being measured from the appearance of an arm, and distinguish a suspected action from a confirmed finding. Accurate language makes the news easier to understand and the discussion fairer to the player.
