Conventional and reverse swing describe sideways movement in flight. The names refer to direction relative to the angled seam, not whether a delivery moves towards a particular batter. A weather percentage alone cannot identify either type.

## Direction and airflow

| Mode | Direction relative to the angled seam |
| --- | --- |
| Conventional | Towards the seam side |
| Reverse | Towards the non-seam side |

The [2024 Cambridge reassessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/flow/article/review-and-reassessment-of-the-aerodynamics-of-cricket-ball-swing/389B2E66A420FF51FAB3EC2186AFE0AE) connects this difference to unequal airflow separation on the two sides. It also revises the simplified account of the seam as a wire that merely triggers turbulence.

## Age alone is not a test

The [Bath research abstract](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/practical-perspective-of-cricket-ball-swing/) reports wind-tunnel tests of balls with match wear. New polished balls showed clearer conventional swing; worn balls produced reverse swing at higher speeds. The paper also found no significant humidity-only effect.

Those findings do not establish a universal over number at which a match ball changes behaviour. Cambridge's experiments also demonstrate reverse swing with new balls under suitable flow conditions. Do not turn “older ball” into a definitive label for an individual delivery.

## Weather needs a narrower claim

Cambridge distinguishes slowly varying atmospheric properties from local wind and turbulence. Temperature and pressure can affect the flow regime; humidity had negligible effects in the reviewed tests. Laboratory turbulence does not settle what occurs above every real pitch.

That supports neither “clouds guarantee swing” nor “weather never matters”. The research explains mechanisms, not a calibrated forecast of wickets from a weather app. It is also not permission to alter a match ball.

## How to use the distinction

Imagine two hypothetical match previews. Both list 80% relative humidity. One then awards every pace bowler extra expected wickets; the other records the weather without changing its player projection. The first has added a numerical conclusion that the humidity reading does not justify. The second leaves room for actual cricket evidence.

A useful observation note separates three questions: what movement was seen, what condition and orientation the ball had, and what is merely forecast. An older ball is not itself an observed reverse-swing delivery. A bowler’s reputation is not a measurement of today’s movement. A promising spell is not a guaranteed wicket in the next over.

For player analysis, retain uncertainty rather than converting one atmospheric percentage into a fixed fantasy uplift. The scientific findings explain mechanisms and challenge shortcuts; they do not supply a calibrated weather-to-wickets model. Ball-allocation rules, pink-ball choices and speed records answer different questions and should remain separate references.
