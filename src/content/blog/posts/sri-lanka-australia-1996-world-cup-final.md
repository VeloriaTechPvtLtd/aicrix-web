Sri Lanka beat Australia by seven wickets in the men's ODI World Cup final at Lahore on 17 March 1996. Australia made 241 for seven; Sri Lanka finished on 245 for three in 46.2 overs. Aravinda de Silva finished unbeaten on 107. Those headline figures describe a successful chase, but they leave out why the middle order mattered so much. [ICC historical finals review](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/six-months-to-cwc-2011-final)

## The victory began before the chase

Australia had reached 137 for one before Sri Lanka pulled the innings back. De Silva's three wickets for 42 included the established pair of Mark Taylor and Ricky Ponting. Sri Lanka ultimately held Australia to 241 for seven, then won their first men's World Cup. [ICC's 1996 tournament overview](https://www.icc-cricket.com/tournaments/cricketworldcup/news/mens-cricket-world-cup-1996-overview)

## Two early wickets did not dictate the ending

De Silva arrived with Sri Lanka at 23 for two. His eventual 107 not out came from 124 balls and included 13 fours. Asanka Gurusinha made 65 from 99 balls; Arjuna Ranatunga finished unbeaten on 47 from 37. These figures show why a description centred only on the opening pair misses the actual shape of the final. [ICC's collection of final centuries](https://www.icc-cricket.com/tournaments/world-test-championship/news/memory-lane-the-best-hundreds-seen-in-icc-tournament-finals)

## Preparation explains more than a claim of fearlessness

In a later ICC interview, de Silva explained that he had prepared to bat very early if necessary. His account connected that readiness to the freedom given to Sri Lanka's openers. He also emphasised Gurusinha's ability to consolidate and described the win as a team achievement. [ICC's 2010 retrospective interview](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/cwc-1996-final-a-huge-moment-says-de-silva)

That recollection offers a useful way to understand the batting order. Backing aggressive players need not mean assuming they will succeed every time. A middle order can prepare for their early dismissal without undermining the attacking plan. The contingency is part of the strategy, not an admission that the strategy has failed.

## Read the result without changing its meaning

The target was 242, not 241. Sri Lanka's finishing total of 245 does not change that target: the scorecard records the total reached at the conclusion of the chase. And 46.2 overs means 46 completed overs plus two legal deliveries, not 46.2 as a decimal number. That leaves 22 legal balls from a 50-over allocation.
