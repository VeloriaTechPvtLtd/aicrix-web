A ball crossing the boundary in the air is not automatically six, and a fielder being airborne is not automatically safe. A boundary catch depends on where the fielder last touched the ground, what happens at every contact with the ball, and how the fielding sequence finishes.

This explanation uses the MCC Laws effective from 1 October 2026. The ICC introduced the revised boundary-catching approach into international playing conditions during 2025, before its inclusion in the MCC code. For a particular competition or older match, use the regulations in force on that date. [MCC implementation notice](https://www.lords.org/lords/news-stories/mcc-prepares-for-new-edition-of-laws-from-1-october-2026) · [ICC announcement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/several-changes-made-to-icc-playing-conditions)

## Start with the first touch

If a fielder is airborne on their first contact with the ball, they must have taken off from wholly inside the boundary. Jumping from outside does not become legal merely because the fielder is no longer touching the ground when the ball arrives. That first-contact test applies to each participating fielder, including someone receiving a relay.

Touching the rope while touching the live ball also creates a boundary problem. And holding the ball for an instant is not necessarily a completed catch: the catcher must control both the ball and their movement. The relevant provisions are Laws 19.4, 19.5 and 33 in the [current MCC code](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

## What changed for a fielder who goes outside

After an initially lawful touch, a fielder can step outside and jump to make one further airborne contact from outside. They must then land and stay wholly inside until the ball is dead. Passing the ball to a teammate does not remove that requirement. Repeated outside jumps cannot keep the same delivery alive indefinitely. [MCC explanation of the 2026 changes](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/86ac7ad2-1031-4e2b-bdea-ff407e7b0db0/Ten-major-changes-to-Laws-for-2026-edition_6.pdf)

The useful question is therefore not simply “Were the feet off the ground?” Track the whole sequence: first touch, any excursion outside, the next airborne touch, then the landing. A television replay that ends at the spectacular fingertip contact can omit the decisive moment.

## Four worked examples

**A clean leap:** A boundary fielder takes off from inside, holds a lofted shot and lands securely inside without touching the rope. Assuming the delivery and catch meet the other requirements, crossing the vertical plane of the boundary while airborne does not by itself invalidate the catch.

**The wrong starting position:** A teammate is standing beyond the rope, jumps and makes their first contact with the same ball. Landing inside afterwards cannot repair the outside take-off. Compare this with the first example: the airborne photograph might look similar, but the preceding foot position changes the decision.

**One recovery:** A fielder legally parries the ball from inside, steps outside, jumps once to send it back, and lands inside before a teammate completes the catch. They remain inside. This sequence can satisfy the boundary conditions; the rules do not prohibit every relay or every second contact.

**A failed recovery:** Change just one detail in that sequence: after the outside-jump touch, the assisting fielder lands outside. The teammate’s secure catch does not rescue the play. The landing condition still applies to the assisting fielder.

These are hypothetical examples, not rulings on named historical catches. They isolate one variable at a time, which is more useful than deciding legality from how athletic a play looks.

## A replay checklist

Check the first touch by each fielder, the final grounded position before that touch, any rope contact, and the landing after a permitted outside jump. Then check whether the ball was held fairly before touching the ground. Only after that should the replay answer “caught” or “boundary.”

An old clip may have been correctly judged under an earlier version of the rules. A current explainer should not silently apply the 2026 wording retrospectively, or label every airborne boundary effort a banned “bunny hop.”
