---
title: "Hit the ball twice rule and protecting the wicket"
excerpt: "Understand lawful second strikes to protect the wicket, the limits around catches and returning the ball, and why a defensive second hit earns no runs."
status: draft
---

**Touching the ball twice is not automatically a dismissal.** The important questions are whether the second contact was deliberate, what it was intended to do, whether a fielder had touched the ball and whether another dismissal rule applies.

A lawful second stroke can protect the wicket. It is not a second opportunity to score.

## What the dismissal covers

Under [MCC Law 34.1](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), the striker can be out when, after contact with bat or body, they deliberately strike the live ball again before a fielder touches it, other than solely to guard the wicket. The provision excludes a hand not holding the bat, which requires the separate obstruction rules.

That makes “double contact” an incomplete description. An accidental deflection from pad to bat is not, merely because two contacts occurred, the deliberate second act described here. Equally, the first contact need not have been a scoring shot with the bat.

Imagine two fictional deliveries. In one, the ball rebounds from a pad onto a stationary bat. In the other, the batter sees the ball lying safely away from the wicket and deliberately hits it towards a gap to collect runs. The second event has an intentional additional stroke and a scoring purpose; the first does not. Counting contacts alone would miss the distinction.

## Protect the wicket without preventing a catch

The [October 2026 ICC men’s Test conditions, clause 34.3](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf), permit a second or later strike solely to guard the wicket, before fielder contact, using the bat or body other than a hand not holding the bat. This protection also applies to a no-ball.

Suppose a legal delivery drops from the bat, bounces and rolls back towards the stumps. The striker uses the held bat only to stop it hitting them. That is the defensive situation the exception addresses, assuming no other infringement.

Now change the facts: the ball has not grounded, and a deliberate second stroke prevents the striker being caught. Defending the wicket does not authorize preventing that catch from a legal delivery. Law 37 must also be considered. A lawful second strike can itself still produce a fair catch if the necessary conditions are met. [MCC Laws 33.2.2.2, 34.3 and 37.3](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

The delivery’s no-ball status matters to the catch exception. Do not turn this legal-delivery example into a blanket rule for every second stroke from a no-ball.

## A permitted defensive stroke does not earn a boundary

Consider a fictional score of 80/2. A legal ball is lawfully struck again solely to protect the wicket, and that defensive stroke sends it to the rope. Assume no catch, dismissal or penalty.

The score remains 80/2, not 84/2. If the batters instead complete one run after the lawful defensive second stroke, that running run is not added either. Neither the rope nor the completed run converts the defensive exception into a scoring stroke.

Clause 34.4 disallows the runs and returns unbeaten batters to their original ends. The umpire’s dead-ball timing preserves any opportunity for a catch. Applicable no-ball and specified penalty awards are treated separately; the rule excludes the fielding-helmet penalty. [Current ICC conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf).

For the same hypothetical rope event on a no-ball, with no other event or penalty, the one-run no-ball award gives 81/2, not 85/2. Keep the penalty separate from the disallowed boundary. This is not a free-hit example or permission to make a deliberate second scoring stroke.

## Returning the ball is a different decision

A return to a fielder is excluded from the hit-the-ball-twice dismissal, but that is not blanket permission to intervene. Returning a live ball without a fielder’s consent can instead be obstructing the field. Likewise, deliberate contact after a fielder has touched the ball falls outside Law 34 but can still need an obstruction assessment. [MCC Laws 34.2 and 37.4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

Do not collapse those statements into “not out.” They identify which provision applies.

Finally, a hit-the-ball-twice dismissal can occur from a no-ball, and the bowler does not receive wicket credit. [ICC clauses 34.1.3 and 34.5](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf). Record the actual ruling rather than treating every team wicket as a bowler’s wicket or every second touch as an offence.
