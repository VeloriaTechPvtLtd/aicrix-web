The international leg-side wide change is no longer merely a trial. The ICC permanently added it to ODI and T20I playing conditions in October 2026, with the updated conditions applying to series starting on or after 1 October. A series already underway keeps its previous regulations.

The central idea is to assess the batter’s leg position at delivery, not simply the gap between the ball and the batter after a late move across the crease. That matters when a batter shuffles toward the off side and a delivery passes through the space they have just vacated. [ICC October 2026 announcement](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/key-changes-across-formats-with-new-icc-playing-conditions)

## What the extra line is for

In its original explanation, the ICC described extending the protected-area marking to the popping crease as an umpiring guide. A ball passing between leg stump and that marker was not to be called wide under the revised leg-side approach. A delivery passing behind the batter’s legs and outside the reference line could still be wide. [ICC explanation of the trial that preceded adoption](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/several-changes-made-to-icc-playing-conditions)

The marker is not a new stump, a new crease that the batter must occupy, or evidence that every leg-side delivery is now legal. It helps identify a reference area. Its appearance on a broadcast should be read with the applicable competition rules, rather than treated as a universal red-line rule for all cricket.

## Separate movement before and after delivery

Consider two hypothetical batters facing an otherwise similar line. The first is already standing farther across when the bowler delivers. The second starts conventionally and moves across afterwards. A freeze-frame taken only as the ball reaches the wicket can hide that difference.

The release frame matters because it establishes the position specified by the rule. The later frame still helps show the ball’s path and any contact, but it cannot replace the release frame. A replay should preserve both rather than infer the starting position from where the batter finishes.

This does not mean that “the batter moved” settles every appeal. The wider conditions also consider reach with a normal cricket stroke, the normal batting position, contact and whether the delivery is a no-ball. Under the [October 2026 men’s T20I conditions, clauses 22.1–22.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf), a wide is not awarded simply because the batter chose not to hit a reachable delivery.

## Three practical checks

**A late move toward the off side:** Do not decide solely from the distance behind the batter when the ball passes. Identify the batter’s position at delivery and then apply the leg-side test. The late movement does not automatically convert the original line into a wide.

**Contact on a thin edge or pad:** A ball touching the striker’s bat or person is not treated as a wide under the relevant contact provisions. That is a separate check from whether a spectator thought the line looked too wide. The resulting batting or extras entry depends on what actually happened.

**A no-ball on the same delivery:** No-ball takes precedence over wide. The scorebook does not add a separate one-run wide penalty simply because the no-ball also travelled far outside the batter. A wide is also not the same as a free hit; that question belongs to the no-ball provisions.

## Why different competitions can look different

An ODI, a T20 international and a franchise-league match are not interchangeable rule labels. The ICC announcement settles the international adoption date; it does not make every league’s wide interpretation identical.

For any disputed delivery, start with the match and series dates, then inspect the relevant conditions and the full replay. A single slogan such as “anything down leg is wide” is no longer a dependable explanation of the current international change.
