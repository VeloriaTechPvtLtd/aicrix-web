A run between wickets is a shared action, even when only one batter has struck the ball. One player can see a gap and be ready to move while the other is still trying to locate the ball. Calling is the bridge between those two views.

Good communication does not make every proposed single safe. It makes the decision understandable to both partners. That distinction is useful whether you are learning to bat, reviewing a run-out or watching an established partnership work.

## Three short calls, one shared meaning

New Zealand Cricket's introductory coaching manual teaches three calls: yes, no and wait. It encourages clear, audible communication and connects sound calling with running between wickets and rotating the strike. This is coaching guidance, not a separate law that decides which batter is dismissed. [NZC Transition to Hard Ball manual, batting section](https://play.nzc.nz/sites/default/files/2026-02/Transition%20to%20Hard%20Ball.pdf)

The words are deliberately short. In a partnership discussion, it is useful to agree what each means in practice: a positive decision, a refusal, or a temporary hold while information develops. “Wait” should not be silently interpreted as “yes” by one partner and “no” by the other.

Those explanations are a practical communication framework rather than a promise that a fixed vocabulary eliminates mistakes. Two batters can use the same words and still differ in what they believe has been agreed.

## Readiness is not permission to leave early

The same NZC manual encourages readiness for a run and backing up. That should not be read as an instruction to leave the ground before it is legally safe to do so. The timing and dismissal questions belong to the applicable laws and playing conditions; a coaching cue cannot replace them.

There is a useful mental distinction here. “Be ready to run” describes attention and preparation. “Run now” describes a decision. Confusing the two can turn anticipation into an unwanted commitment.

For a new partnership, discussing this distinction before a ball is delivered is more useful than trying to settle it during a hurried exchange halfway down the pitch.

## Three situations that sound similar but are not

Consider these fictional examples.

First, a batter pushes the ball towards a nearby fielder and calls no immediately. The partner stays home. No run has been scored, but the communication has still done useful work: both players have reached the same decision.

Second, a ball travels into space while a fielder moves across. A temporary wait allows the pair to assess the developing situation. If a clear decision follows, the pause can be part of the communication rather than a failure of it. Whether a run remains available depends on what actually happens, not on the word alone.

Third, one batter takes several steps without an audible call while the partner watches the ball. Even if they ultimately complete the single, the successful result does not prove that the process was clear. The same mismatch could matter more against a quicker collection.

These examples illustrate communication, not recommended runs against particular field positions. The speed of the ball, fielders, throw and both batters still matter.

## The second run is another decision

Completing one run does not automatically settle whether two are available. Treat the possibility of another run as fresh information to communicate, rather than assuming the first call covers every subsequent movement.

A partnership can discuss who has the clearer view in different situations, but avoid turning a simple convention into a claim that one person always has perfect information. Both players remain responsible for noticing when their understanding differs.

An original review checklist is helpful after practice: was a call made, was it heard, did both partners understand it, and did their movement match that understanding? This separates a communication problem from a physical or judgement problem without pretending they never interact.

## Calling and dismissal are separate questions

When two batters finish at the same end, spectators often ask who made the bad call. The rules question is different: which batter was out of their ground when the wicket was legally broken? The applicable run-out and ground-ownership rules decide that question, not a judgment about who called badly. See [MCC Laws 30 and 38](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf) and our [same-end run-out explainer](/blog/both-batters-same-end-run-out).

Blame is not an umpiring test. Nor does the batter who gets dismissed necessarily supply the whole explanation of what went wrong. A fair review reconstructs the sequence before assigning responsibility.

## Build a partnership habit, not a slogan

The aim is not to shout constantly without listening. It is to create a common understanding quickly enough for both batters to act.

For coached practice, begin with communication at an appropriate level of difficulty rather than copying risky professional singles or imposing arbitrary sprint targets. Clear calls, shared expectations and calm review offer a more useful starting point than demanding that a pair simply “run harder.”
