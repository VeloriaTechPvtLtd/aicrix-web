excerpt: "Track bowling arm and over-or-round mode separately, with a six-ball communication example."

## Yes, but the change must be communicated

A bowler can use different arms within the same over. The important requirement is notification: under **MCC’s 2026 Law 21.1.1**, the umpire establishes whether the bowler is right- or left-handed and bowling over or round the wicket, then informs the striker. An unannounced change of that mode is a no-ball. [MCC 2026 Laws](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf)

The rule is not “pick one arm for six balls.” Nor does using the other arm automatically make a delivery illegal. A bowler planning to switch should make the change clear to the umpire before delivering, rather than relying on the batter to notice a different run-up.

## Bowling arm and side are separate details

“Right-arm over” contains two pieces of information. The first identifies the delivery arm; the second identifies the bowler’s relationship to the wicket. Under Appendix A.9.1, the wicket is on the bowling-arm side during an over-the-wicket approach. For round the wicket, the return crease is on that side instead.

Consequently, “I am staying over the wicket” does not answer whether the arm has changed. Likewise, moving round the wicket is a mode change even if the bowler keeps using the right arm. A clear call includes both details, not just “same as before” when one has changed.

## A six-ball example

The following is an invented sequence. All six deliveries are assumed valid; the table illustrates communication, not a real match or a tactical recommendation.

| Intended delivery | Mode | Change from preceding delivery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Right-arm over | Initial mode established |
| 2 | Right-arm over | None |
| 3 | Left-arm over | Arm changes |
| 4 | Left-arm round | Side changes |
| 5 | Left-arm round | None |
| 6 | Right-arm round | Arm changes |

There are three changes after the initial mode has been established. Treat the first announcement and the later changes as separate events. A record saying only “bowled both arms” would not reveal whether the bowler also changed sides.

## Kamindu Mendis provides a real example

The ICC’s [8 January 2018 interview with Kamindu Mendis](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/a-true-all-rounder-meet-ambidextrous-spinner-kamindu-mendis) described him bowling spin with both arms and sometimes switching mid-over. He explained that he told the umpire and made the batter aware of the change.

The interview also described a usual preference for left-arm bowling to a right-hander and the opposite combination against a left-hander, while making clear that the situation could alter his choice. It is a historical example of the skill and communication, not proof of what he bowls to every opponent today.

## Do not confuse a mode with every variation

Changing from a leg break to another variation is not necessarily a change of arm or over/round mode. Law 21.1’s notification categories should not be read as a requirement to announce every intended variation. Equally, calling a delivery “spin” does not excuse an unannounced switch of arm.

For a specific incident, identify the old mode, the new mode and whether the required notification occurred. Check any competition-specific conditions as well. A replay showing an unfamiliar action alone does not establish which information the umpire and striker received.
