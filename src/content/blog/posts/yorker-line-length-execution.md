A yorker is a very full delivery that reaches the batter around the feet and beneath the bat. Cornwall Cricket’s introductory guide describes it as landing close to the toes, just under or behind the bat. The defining feature is its length, not whether it produces a wicket. [Cornwall Cricket glossary](https://cornwallcricket.co.uk/uploads/assets/5d7d0663-4195-410d-aa04a94201ccc162/A-Simple-Guide-to-Cricket.pdf).

That distinction helps explain a familiar commentary phrase: an “attempted yorker.” A bowler can aim for that length without achieving it. To analyse the ball fairly, separate the intended plan, the delivery that actually arrived and the resulting score.

## A yorker can target different lines

In a 2016 ECB interview, Chris Jordan described practising wide, straight and leg-stump yorkers with separate targets. He also used a different target for a slower ball. The example shows that “yorker” does not identify one compulsory line or one complete bowling plan. [Jordan’s target drill](https://www.ecb.co.uk/news/112744/chris-jordan-on-how-to-bowl-yorkers).

A straight target and a target outside off stump should therefore not be treated as interchangeable when reviewing a spell. Record the line as well as the length. If a replay is too unclear to locate the bounce, leave that classification uncertain rather than deciding from the batter’s reaction.

Calling a delivery a wide yorker is a technical description of its intended line. Whether the umpire calls a Wide is a separate adjudication under the conditions of the match. The adjective alone is not the decision.

## The batter can change the encounter

Century Cricket Coaching’s demonstration offers several responses: moving back to meet an intended yorker as a half-volley, moving forward to intercept it as a full toss, or using the bat’s angle to guide the ball rather than attempting a large swing. These are demonstrated options, not promises that every batter can execute them against every delivery. [Batting against the yorker](https://australiancricketinstitute.com/how-to-score-off-the-yorker/).

This is why a pitch map alone can leave part of the story untold. To understand what the batter faced, watch where the batter was when contact occurred, not only the point at which the ball was projected to land.

For example, imagine a batter advancing before a very full ball arrives and meeting it before it bounces. Describing only the bowler’s original target would miss the actual contact. Conversely, a batter remaining deep in the crease may encounter the same intended length differently. These are illustrative situations, not data from a particular match.

## Accuracy includes reacting without losing the target

The ECB account describes Jordan’s coach changing the target shortly before delivery to simulate a batter moving. Jordan explained the need to adjust his line while retaining execution. The drill is a useful example of practising response as well as repetition. [ECB training account](https://www.ecb.co.uk/news/112744/chris-jordan-on-how-to-bowl-yorkers).

Do not turn one professional’s session into a universal workload prescription. Its analytical value is the distinction between bowling at a stationary marker and maintaining control when the situation changes.

When watching a match, ask whether the batter moved, whether the bowler visibly adjusted and where the ball ultimately arrived. Avoid claiming to know the bowler’s thought process unless a first-person explanation is available.

## Judge a sequence rather than a highlight

Consider a fictional six-ball sequence containing several well-executed yorkers and one delivery that misses its intended length. A compilation showing only the successful balls cannot tell you the cost of the whole over. A compilation showing only the boundary cannot tell you how often the plan worked.

A useful review logs every delivery: intended option where known, observed line and length, batter movement, contact and outcome. Keep extras and wickets visible rather than reducing the sequence to a flattering selection of clips.

The same principle applies when comparing bowlers. A reputation for yorkers is not a measured success rate. A measured rate needs a clear definition of success and a complete denominator of attempts, including errors.

## What the label can and cannot tell you

The yorker label tells you about a type of delivery. It does not establish that the ball was unplayable, that a boundary was impossible or that the bowler should use it every time.

For match and fantasy analysis, treat the skill as one part of the player’s role, not a guarantee of wickets or points. The practical takeaway is simple: identify the length, add the line and the batter’s position, then assess the full sequence. That produces a better explanation than calling every full ball a yorker or every missed target a bad plan.
