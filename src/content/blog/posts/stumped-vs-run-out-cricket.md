A wicketkeeper can complete either a stumping or a run-out. The player removing the bails does not, by itself, identify the dismissal. Equally, a batter need not be attempting a run to be run out.

Under the [MCC 2026 Laws 38–39](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf), stumping concerns the striker, out of their ground without attempting a run, from a delivery not called no-ball. The keeper must fairly break the wicket without another fielder intervening. If all stumping conditions hold, that classification takes priority over run-out.

This guide compares ordinary cases without runners. Each example assumes the ball remains live, the batter is genuinely out of their ground, the wicket is fairly broken and an appeal is made. Those assumptions isolate the classification question rather than decide an incomplete replay.

## Five scenes with one fact changed

| Fictional scene | Classification under the stated assumptions |
| --- | --- |
| A advances to play a normal delivery, misses and attempts no run; keeper alone breaks the wicket | Stumped |
| A misses but then attempts a single; keeper alone breaks A’s wicket before A makes ground | Run out, not stumped |
| Repeat the first scene, but the delivery is a no-ball | Neither stumped nor run out by that keeper-only action |
| Repeat the attempted-single scene on a no-ball | Run out remains available |
| A remains outside the crease without attempting a run; a slip fielder returns the live ball to the keeper, who breaks A’s wicket | Run out, not stumped |

These comparisons follow [ICC men’s Test clauses 38.1, 38.2.2.2 and 39.1](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf), effective October 2026. No competing dismissal, injury exception or completed match is assumed.

In the first two scenes, the keeper’s final movement can look identical. The changed fact is A’s attempt to run. In the first and fifth, there is no attempted run in either scene; another fielder’s participation changes the available classification. That is why a close-up of the gloves and bails cannot answer the entire question.

## Why a no ball is not an automatic run out

A no-ball prevents stumping. It also protects a striker outside their ground who is not attempting a run when the keeper alone breaks the wicket. Relabelling that action “run out” cannot bypass the exception. But that combination of facts is narrower than immunity from every run-out on a no-ball. [MCC Law 38.2.2.2](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

The fourth scene deliberately changes the protected fact: A tries to run. The third and fourth therefore cannot be combined into “a no-ball means safe” or “a no-ball only changes the dismissal’s name.” Identify the action before assigning a label.

## The keeper does not always need to remove the bails by hand

The wicket can count as broken by the keeper when the ball rebounds from their body or equipment, or is kicked or thrown onto the stumps by them. Those routes do not alone turn a stumping into a run-out; the other conditions still matter. [ICC clause 39.2](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf)

Think of a missed shot followed by a rebound from a keeper’s pad. The relevant questions remain whether the striker attempted a run, whether another fielder intervened and whether the delivery permits stumping. A catch-and-sweep motion is not the definition.

## Keep exceptions separate from the ordinary cases

Leaving the ground after receiving the delivery to avoid injury has specific stumping protection. Run-out has its own injury-avoidance wording. A wide, unlike a no-ball, can permit stumping, subject to the match still continuing. [MCC Laws 38.2, 39.3 and 39.4](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf)

For a real incident, record the delivery status, striker’s action, each fielder’s involvement and the moment the wicket broke. Then apply the governing competition’s conditions. Whether the wicket was fairly broken and who faces next are separate checks, not reasons to change the dismissal label.
