A spinner can trouble a batter before the ball touches the pitch. Drift changes its sideways path in the air; dip changes its descent. Turn describes what happens after the bounce. Keeping those stages separate makes a replay much easier to read, especially when a delivery takes a wicket without a dramatic sideways break. ## Three questions for one delivery

Start with the flight: does the ball move sideways relative to its initial path? That is the question behind drift. Then consider where it lands relative to the trajectory the batter appeared to anticipate. Dip can make the eventual length shorter than an otherwise comparable delivery without that spin effect. Finally, follow the ball after contact with the surface to examine turn.

Ian and Garry Robinson explain these possibilities in their account of their mathematical research on spinning balls and wind. Their model allows combinations of sideways drift and vertical movement. It is not a promise that every spinning ball will follow one identical curve: the model also makes simplifying assumptions, including ignoring the seam. [Researchers’ explanation](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/comment-could-the-physics-of-spin-bowling-turn-the-ashes-around/6uteknelx)

For a viewer, the useful habit is chronological. Do not start with the wicket and work backwards towards a favourite description. Watch the release, the journey, the bounce and the batter’s contact as separate events.

## A high trajectory is not a complete diagnosis

A ball that travels visibly high gives an obvious impression of flight. That alone does not establish how much dip or drift it had. Describing something as beautifully flighted can be a reasonable judgement, but explaining the judgement needs more detail.

Ask what the trajectory made the batter do. Did the front foot commit early? Did the bat reach forward while the ball remained farther away than expected? Was the batter still balanced enough to change the shot? Those observations offer a more useful explanation than treating height as a score for quality.

Equally, a flatter-looking delivery is not automatically harmless. The comparison should be with the surrounding deliveries and the batter’s response, not with a single ideal silhouette.

## What a spinning stock ball contributes

New Zealand Cricket’s coaching manual encourages developing spinners to establish an accurate, hard-spinning stock delivery before concentrating on variations. It links dip and drift to revolutions and release orientation, and encourages experimentation with different proportions of side spin and top spin. [NZC coaching manual, spin-bowling curriculum](https://play.nzc.nz/sites/default/files/2024-10/NZC_Dev_Coaching%20Course_2024v2.pdf)

That guidance suggests a useful distinction for analysis: variation is not only a separately named delivery. A familiar ball can create different problems when its trajectory or landing point changes. Before announcing that a bowler has unveiled a mystery weapon, look for evidence of a different release or movement rather than relying only on the batter’s mistake.

## An illustrative sequence

Imagine a fictional over in which the first two deliveries invite comfortable forward defensive strokes. On the third, the batter advances a similar distance but finds the ball landing beyond the bat’s reach. A wicketkeeper completes a stumping.

That result invites several possible explanations. Perhaps the third ball dipped more. Perhaps its original length was shorter. Perhaps the batter moved earlier or farther. A replay should help distinguish those possibilities; the dismissal itself cannot.

Now imagine a fourth delivery that turns sharply but is safely left alone. It may be the most visibly spectacular ball of the over without having created its most difficult decision. This is why movement and effectiveness deserve separate descriptions.

## Keep an honest viewing notebook

For a short spell, record four things: apparent flight, landing area, movement after bounce and the batter’s response. Use words such as uncertain when the camera angle obscures one stage. A note that says “reached forward and missed” is more defensible than inventing a numerical measure of dip from television pictures.

The central lesson is simple: watch the whole delivery. Drift, dip and turn answer different questions, and none needs an invented measurement to make a good cricket explanation.
