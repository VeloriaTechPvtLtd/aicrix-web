DLS par score and a revised target answer different questions. **Par is the score that would leave a chase level at a specified interruption point. A revised target is the total required to win over the remaining allocated innings.** A team can be ahead of live par without having finished its chase.

The distinction matters most when a match stops. Under the ICC’s October 2026 men’s conditions, an eligible chase that cannot resume is compared with par at the suspension. A chase that resumes uses the officially revised target. [ODI clause 16.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf).

### Why wickets matter as well as overs

Duckworth–Lewis–Stern adjusts for the scoring resources available to each side. Overs remaining and wickets lost both matter; a simple comparison of run rates misses part of that picture. The ICC’s [methodology note](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/orlbya4cqyhqaceje3b2.pdf) also separates professional DLS software from the older D/L Standard Edition explained in its manual tables.

Think about two otherwise similar interruptions at 100/1 and 100/7. The scores and balls faced are equal, but the batting resources are not. That is why a rain benchmark should travel with its exact ball and wicket state, rather than appearing as an isolated number.

### A worked reading example without inventing DLS calculations

Suppose the officials provide a par score of 120 at the precise point play stops. These are hypothetical scores used to illustrate the comparison, not outputs from a reconstructed DLS model.

| Chasing score when play stops | Comparison with supplied par 120 | If this becomes an eligible final DLS result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 124 | Four above par | Chasing team wins by four runs |
| 120 | Equal to par | Scores are tied under DLS |
| 117 | Three below par | Chasing team loses by three runs |

For a hypothetical T20I version of this example, suppose play instead resumes with an official target of 151 in 18 overs. The objective is 151, not the old live-par figure plus one. The old benchmark described a different stopping point. Reaching 121 does not complete this revised chase.

### Use the exact ball not the next over

Some scoring displays show an end-of-over par benchmark while the over is in progress. ECB’s [scoring guidance](https://play-cricket.ecb.co.uk/hc/en-us/articles/360000606789-Duckworth-Lewis-The-5-Most-Common-Examples-PCS-Pro) says to replace that with the exact-ball figure if the players leave mid-over. A displayed value for the completed over is not a reliable substitute for the value at the actual stoppage.

For example, 12.3 overs means 12 overs and three legal balls, or 75 balls. It is not 12.3 multiplied by six. Record the wicket count at that point too. Both pieces prevent a correct number from being attached to the wrong match state.

### Minimum overs and a tied score

For ordinary men’s international conditions, the usual minimum opportunity is 20 overs per side in ODIs and five in T20Is, with exceptions for an earlier all-out innings or successful chase. Tournament-specific rules still need checking. A tied DLS comparison is not automatically a final tied match: the applicable tie-break procedure may follow. [ODI clauses 16.1–16.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bxkzkcyvfn6tvi70zh0w.pdf); [T20I clauses 16.1–16.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf).

### What a useful live rain update should show

Read the official method, score and wickets, legal balls completed, revised allocation, target or par label, and update time together. A rain delay is not itself a no-result announcement. Nor should an unofficial calculator replace the match officials’ target.

Keep three concepts separate: DLS par is a rain-result benchmark; venue par is an estimate of a competitive score; net run rate is a standings calculation. They may all involve runs and overs, but they are not interchangeable.
