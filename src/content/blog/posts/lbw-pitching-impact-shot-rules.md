LBW means leg before wicket, but a ball striking the pad is not enough for dismissal. The umpire has to connect the delivery’s legality, its landing point, contact with the bat, the point of impact and its projected path to the stumps. A failure at one required step can make the batter not out even when another step looks obvious.

Under [MCC Law 36 in the 2026 edition](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf), the delivery must not be a no-ball; it must not have touched the bat before the body; and it must have been going on to hit the wicket. If it bounces before impact, it must first land in line with the wickets or on the off side, not outside leg stump.

## Landing and impact are different places

Landing is where the ball first bounces. Impact is where it first strikes the batter’s body. Saying only “outside the line” leaves out which of those two events you mean.

For impact, a genuine attempt to play the ball with the bat normally requires contact in line with the wickets. If no genuine shot is attempted, impact outside off stump can also qualify. Impact outside leg stump does not receive that exception. [Law 36.1.4](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf).

## Four situations that are easy to confuse

| Situation | LBW consequence, assuming the remaining tests are satisfied |
| --- | --- |
| Ball lands outside leg, then swings or spins toward middle stump | Not out LBW; its later path does not repair the landing failure |
| Ball lands outside off, strikes in line, and would hit the stumps | Can be out; outside-off landing is permitted |
| Ball strikes outside off while a genuine shot is attempted | Not out LBW on the impact test |
| Ball strikes outside off with no genuine attempt to play it | Can be out; the no-shot exception affects impact |

These are simplified teaching cases, not decisions about a particular replay. The point is to test each condition separately. “It was hitting the stumps” answers only the final trajectory question, not all the earlier ones. The landing-versus-impact distinction is also set out in the [ICC’s clause 36 explainer](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/mens-odi-match-clause-36-leg-before-wicket), consistent with the current rule on these points.

## Does it have to hit a leg

No. Law 36 covers interception by any part of the batter’s person. A full toss can also produce LBW because the law does not require the ball to bounce before impact. Only the first interception is assessed. Simultaneous bat-and-body contact is treated by MCC as bat first. [Law 36.1–36.2](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf).

That last point is why the sequence of contacts matters more than a loud pad impact. An inside edge before the pad changes the case; a ball striking the body first cannot be excused merely because it later touches the bat.

## What DRS adds

Where DRS is in use, the ICC protocol checks delivery legality and bat contact before using ball tracking for landing, impact and the wicket. Umpire’s Call identifies marginal impact or wicket assessments; it is not a general label for every uncertain event. [ICC men’s T20I Appendix D, section 3.4](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/bsxcho7ufe2pjban0wjp.pdf).

A replay discussion is clearer when it names the disputed test: bat first, landing outside leg, impact outside off with a shot, or missing the stumps. The applicable review protocol then determines whether the evidence changes the original decision.

## A simple way to follow an appeal

Work from the start of the ball’s journey toward the wicket, rather than beginning with the final animation. Check legality, landing if any, the first contact, the shot and impact, then the likely path. Keep the governing competition’s playing conditions alongside MCC’s Laws when watching a reviewed decision.
