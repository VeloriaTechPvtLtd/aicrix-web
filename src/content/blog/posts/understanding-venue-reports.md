A ground’s average first-innings score is a summary of selected past innings. It can provide a useful starting point for match analysis, but it cannot by itself tell you what will be competitive today.

To use the number well, check which matches produced it, how many there were and whether their conditions resemble the upcoming game.

## Ask what the average includes

“Average score: 170” leaves several important questions unanswered.

Is that a first-innings average or an average across both innings? Does it cover T20 internationals, a domestic league or several competitions together? Does it refer to men’s or women’s matches? Were shortened innings included? When does the record start and end?

A useful venue card answers those questions beside the number.

Cricsheet’s [match-data format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json/) distinguishes fields such as venue, format, competition, gender, dates and innings. Those distinctions make it possible to define a sample clearly. Combining them without explanation makes a venue headline harder to interpret.

## Work through a small example

Imagine five first innings from full-length T20 matches at a fictional ground:

120, 150, 160, 170 and 200.

The total is 800, so the mean is 160. The middle score, or median, is also 160.

Now add a sixth first innings of 260. The mean becomes about 176.7, while the median becomes 165.

No new physical information about the pitch has been added to this example. One score has simply changed the summary of a small dataset.

That does not mean the high score should be discarded. It means you should look beyond one average. A venue report is more informative when it shows the sample count, the individual results or a distribution, and an explanation of unusual matches.

There is also no reason to announce that 177 has suddenly become the ground’s precise par score. The average describes the chosen sample; a forecast needs a separate argument.

## Separate first innings from successful chases

In an ordinary successful chase, the batting side stops once the winning result is reached. For example, clause 16.5.1 of the [ICC Women’s T20I playing conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/vc9oqfirkjvjvvk5nccy.pdf) specifies that the match ends when the result is achieved.

That matters when comparing innings totals.

A team finishing at 141 after an opposition first innings of 140 has not necessarily shown that 141 was the most it could score in the allotted overs. Its task was completed. Treating that total as directly equivalent to a first innings played out to its conclusion can lead to a misleading comparison.

Read first-innings summaries separately. For chasing analysis, consider the target, result, balls remaining and wickets rather than comparing raw innings totals alone.

Shortened matches need their own treatment too. A total from a ten-over innings should not silently enter a headline intended to describe a standard twenty-over first innings.

## Understand the two meanings of par

In ordinary match discussion, “par” is often an analyst’s view of a competitive score in the conditions. Ask how that view was formed and whether it assumes particular teams, a full innings or a certain state of the chase.

DLS par has a formal role in rain-affected matches. It is not the historical venue average. The [ICC conditions](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/vc9oqfirkjvjvvk5nccy.pdf) discuss DLS targets and par scores under their interrupted-match provisions.

A useful article should make clear which meaning it uses. Labelling a venue mean “DLS par” would give a descriptive statistic a role it does not have.

## Keep the sample relevant without hiding it

Recent matches may better reflect current teams or playing conditions, but using only a few recent games makes the summary sensitive to individual results. A longer sample gives more observations while potentially combining different eras and contexts.

A practical report can show both, with explicit dates and counts. Readers can then see whether the recent record differs from the wider sample.

Separate men’s and women’s records and make the competition scope clear. Where evidence is limited, say how limited it is. Cricsheet’s [coverage documentation](https://cricsheet.org/coverage/) demonstrates why even a substantial database should describe missing matches rather than assume complete coverage.

The same care applies to abandoned games, incomplete records and duplicate venue names. An unexplained filter can change an average as much as a new match.

## Add today’s context

After reading the historical sample, identify what is known about the specific fixture:

- Which strip is being used, if reliably reported.

- The confirmed teams and relevant player roles.

- Match timing and a current weather forecast.

- Whether the game has been shortened.

- Any verified change in playing conditions.

Do not convert those observations into a precise new par score without a defined method. If a report says a surface looks slower, explain the evidence and the uncertainty rather than subtracting an arbitrary number of runs from the historical average.

## Read the venue card in order

Start with the format and competition. Check the gender, dates and number of innings. Read the mean alongside the spread of scores. Separate chases and reduced-overs games. Then consider what is actually known about today’s match.

That sequence makes venue statistics useful without asking them to answer a question they were not calculated to answer.
