Guinness World Records lists **Shoaib Akhtar’s 161.3 km/h delivery** in its fastest-delivery category for men. The Pakistan fast bowler recorded it against England at Newlands, Cape Town, on 22 February 2003 during the World Cup. [Guinness record entry](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/fastest-bowl-of-a-cricket-ball)

That is approximately 100.2 miles per hour. The entry describes an electronically measured delivery, not Akhtar’s average pace or a complete comparison of every ball ever bowled.

## What the contemporary tournament figures show

The ICC’s account of the 2003 World Cup group stage also lists Akhtar at 161.3 km/h. Its figures came from the host broadcaster’s measurements. Brett Lee’s highest reading in that comparison was 160.7 km/h, also against England. [ICC pace report](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/akhtar-and-lee-head-pace-race-at-cwc2003)

Those numbers answer a defined tournament question. They should not be silently relabelled as an exhaustive list of career peaks or today’s fastest active bowlers.

## Kilometres per hour versus miles per hour

Using 1 mile = 1.609344 kilometres, divide the kilometre-per-hour reading by 1.609344:

| Reading | Rounded conversion |
| --- | ---: |
| 150 km/h | 93.2 mph |
| 160 km/h | 99.4 mph |
| 161.3 km/h | 100.2 mph |

For the record reading, the more precise calculation is approximately 100.227 mph, or 100.23 to two decimal places. These are different roundings of one measurement, not competing speed readings. Likewise, 160 km/h is below 100 mph; 100 mph equals 160.9344 km/h.

## Why “recorded” is an important qualification

The Guinness entry preserves the ICC’s caution about differences between speed guns. It also warns that online record pages may not reflect changes immediately. The careful claim is therefore that this is the performance listed in the cited record category, not a guarantee of universally identical measurement conditions.

When comparing another speed claim, check its match, format, measurement source and coverage period. A maximum is the highest measured delivery in a sample; it is not the sample’s average.
