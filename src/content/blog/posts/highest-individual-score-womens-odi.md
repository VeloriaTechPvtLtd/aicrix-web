**Record-table snapshot: 28 October 2026.** This comparison preserves that dated women’s ODI record list rather than claiming a live all-time table.

**Amelia Kerr’s 232 not out is the highest individual score in women’s ODI cricket in the checked record table.** She made it for New Zealand against Ireland in Dublin on 13 June 2018, passing Belinda Clark’s unbeaten 229 for Australia against Denmark in Mumbai on 16 December 1997. Both innings lasted until the end of their team’s allocation. [Guinness record](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/529786-most-runs-scored-by-a-player-in-a-one-day-international-female).

## The two women’s ODI double centuries

| Batter | Score | Balls | Opponent | Match date | Team total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Amelia Kerr, New Zealand | 232 not out | 145 | Ireland | 13 June 2018 | 440/3 |
| Belinda Clark, Australia | 229 not out | 155 | Denmark | 16 December 1997 | 412/3 |

The [record table updated 28 October 2026](https://cricprism.com/cricket-records/odi/womens/highest-individual-scores) contains these two innings at 200 or above; the next entry is Chamari Athapaththu’s 195 not out. This is an inclusive 200-run cutoff, not a top-two list that silently omits another qualifying score. An asterisk in a scorecard means not out, not a reduced or unofficial score.

The table concerns recognised women’s one-day internationals. A domestic List A innings, a women’s Test score or an innings in a T20 competition belongs in a different record category. Likewise, 440 and 412 are team totals, not scores made by Kerr and Clark.

## Kerr’s 232 was part of a larger batting partnership

Kerr opened the innings at Castle Avenue, Dublin. Amy Satterthwaite made 61 before the first wicket fell at 113; Leigh Kasperek then joined Kerr and scored 113. New Zealand finished on 440/3, including 30 extras. The full scorecard records Kerr’s 31 fours and two sixes. [NZC-hosted scorecard](https://archive.nzc.nz/Scorecards/1062/1062404.html).

Kerr and Kasperek added 295, taking the team from 113/1 to 408/2. Their individual final scores add to 345, but that is not the partnership: Kerr had already scored before Kasperek arrived. Comparing the wicket checkpoints avoids attributing those earlier runs to the second-wicket stand.

The stand lasted from 14.1 to 47.2 overs, or 199 legal balls. Its calculated scoring rate was 295 × 6 ÷ 199 = 8.89 per over. That is a team-partnership measure, whereas Kerr’s 232 from 145 produces an individual strike rate of 160.00. The denominators are different, so neither should replace the other.

Kerr also took 5/17 from seven overs. Ireland were dismissed for 135, giving New Zealand a 305-run victory. The bowling return adds an important second part to her performance, but it does not change the definition of the individual batting record. [Cricket Ireland’s contemporary report](https://cricketireland.ie/news/record-breaking-amelia-kerr-leads-new-zealand-white-ferns-to-clean-sweep/).

## Clark made 229 without hitting a six

Clark’s innings came at the Middle Income Group ground in Mumbai during the 1997 World Cup. Her 229 not out from 155 balls included 22 fours and no sixes. Australia reached 412/3 before dismissing Denmark for 49, winning by 363 runs. [Historical scorecard](https://www.womenscricket.net/stats/WorldCup/WC97/05%20m48.htm).

Cricket Australia’s retrospective confirms that this was the first double century in ODI cricket, not merely the first in women’s ODIs. It also highlights a feature that a six-hitting summary would miss: 141 of Clark’s runs did not come from boundaries. Her innings is a reminder that a very large total can be built through sustained scoring between the boundary hits. [Cricket Australia](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/4106318/stories-after-stumps-podcast-cricket-belinda-clark-australia-world-cup-1997-india-229-double-century-mumbai-denmark).

## Similar totals with different scoring patterns

| Calculated measure | Kerr | Clark |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Boundary runs | 136 | 88 |
| Runs excluding fours and sixes | 96 | 141 |
| Boundary share of individual runs | 58.62% | 38.43% |
| Strike rate, runs per 100 balls | 160.00 | 147.74 |
| Share of team total, including extras | 52.73% | 55.58% |

These calculations use the [2018 scorecard](https://archive.nzc.nz/Scorecards/1062/1062404.html) and [1997 scorecard](https://www.womenscricket.net/stats/WorldCup/WC97/05%20m48.htm). Kerr’s boundary runs are 31 × 4 + 2 × 6 = 136; Clark’s are 22 × 4 = 88. Subtracting those amounts from the individual scores gives the non-boundary runs, without inventing an exact count of singles, twos or threes.

Clark supplied a slightly greater share of her team’s total despite making three fewer runs. That is not a contradiction: Australia’s total was lower. The calculation includes team extras in the denominator, so it answers “what share of the scoreboard did this batter supply?”, not “what share of runs credited to batters?”.

## What the record does and does not tell you

Kerr finished three runs above Clark’s mark and faced ten fewer balls. Those are useful comparisons of the two recorded innings, not a controlled test of the batters’ relative ability. Different opponents, grounds and eras remain part of the context.

Both double centuries came in victories, but neither the individual score nor its strike rate alone explains the winning margin. Those margins also depended on the opposition innings. Keep the single-innings record separate from career runs, career centuries and predictions about a future match.
