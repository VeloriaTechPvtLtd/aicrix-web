The large plain panel behind a bowler is not an unfinished advertising board. It is a sight screen: a background intended to help the batter pick out the ball. Its position and colour serve a different purpose from the scoreboard or the stadium’s video screen.

That purpose also explains why a batter may wait while something behind the bowler is adjusted. The issue is the view from the crease, not whether the television picture looks clear.

## What a sight screen does

The ECB’s April 2025 covered-outdoor-facilities design guide describes a screen behind the bowler as necessary for a good visual background. It recommends arrangements that do not billow in the wind and calls for dark material when a white ball is used. Its illustrated guidance specifies white for red-ball use and black for white-ball use. This is facility design guidance, not a single rule covering every international format. [ECB design guide, page 33](https://resources.ecb.co.uk/ecb/document/2025/05/12/85d63ce5-c932-4bc7-b34c-66cf75f9a5ce/Cricket-Dome-Design-Guidance-Note-v1.pdf).

The practical idea is contrast. A suitable background should make the ball easier to distinguish, rather than compete with it visually. But a colour label alone does not tell you that a particular installation works well. The batter’s actual line of sight still matters.

For example, imagine a screen that looks perfectly centred from a camera above the stands. The relevant inspection is from the batting position towards the bowler’s delivery area. Those are different viewpoints. This is a simple perspective example, not a diagnosis of any stadium.

## Does every day-night match need a black screen?

No blanket statement is safe. The checked ICC men’s Test conditions, effective October 2026, require screens at both ends and explicitly allow white or black for day-night Tests, as determined by the Home Board. [ICC Test conditions, Appendix C 2.3.1](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf).

That qualification matters for pink-ball questions. Do not take a white-ball convention and automatically apply it to every fixture played under floodlights. Match format, ball and governing conditions should be identified together.

| Question | What to establish |
| --- | --- |
| Why is this screen dark? | The ball and competition, not just the start time |
| Is a white screen wrong in this day-night Test? | The applicable Test conditions and Home Board’s choice |
| Why does a practice facility use two colours? | Which balls and activities that facility supports |

A useful explanation is therefore more precise than “Tests use white, night matches use black.” The first describes a broad visual association; the second can turn that association into an incorrect universal rule.

## Why advertising sometimes disappears

The ICC Test appendix allows advertising on the screen behind the striker, but requires its removal for the subsequent over from that end. It prohibits flashing or flickering images there and warns against distracting changes. Related perimeter-board provisions address contrasting colours, movement and brightness. [ICC Test conditions, Appendix C 2.2–2.3](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/a8114zro5tkioifz0jqg.pdf).

This separates two uses of a surface: providing commercial display space and supplying an appropriate background for play. An image visible at one moment is not enough to establish a breach. The end being used, timing and applicable restriction all matter.

When reviewing a broadcast clip, record what the board showed and when it changed. Avoid assuming that every advertisement near the screen is prohibited or that every visible change affected a delivery.

## Why play can pause

Readiness and distraction are matters for the umpires, not an automatic entitlement to cancel an unwanted ball. MCC Law 20 distinguishes an unready striker who makes no attempt to play and has an adequate reason from a striker distracted while preparing for or receiving a delivery. [MCC 2026 Laws, 20.4.2.5–20.4.2.6](https://www.lords.org/getattachment/e8b12929-1e84-4927-af4e-2a5875b53589/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29.pdf).

For a sight-screen interruption, first establish whether the concern was raised before the delivery or arose during it, and what the umpire decided. A spectator’s movement, a screen adjustment and an advertising change should not be treated as interchangeable events without evidence.

Whether the delivery counts is a separate scoring question under Law 20. Here, the key is simpler: maintaining the visual background and deciding the status of a delivery are related but distinct tasks.

## A spectator’s practical checklist

If you are near a screen, follow the steward’s instructions about movement and access. Do not move equipment yourself or assume an apparently empty space is available for standing.

When a delay occurs, distinguish what you can see from what has been explained. “The batter asked for an adjustment” is an observation. “The screen caused the previous dismissal” is a causal claim requiring much stronger evidence.

For clubs reviewing their facilities, take the issue to the ground team and officials. Start with the intended ball, playing use and view from the crease rather than choosing equipment solely by a photograph or colour description.

A sight screen is a small part of the ground with a specific visual job. Understanding that job makes both the colour choices and the pauses easier to follow, without inventing performance effects or treating every match as governed by the same specification.
