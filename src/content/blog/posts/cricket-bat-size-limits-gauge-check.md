title: "Cricket bat size limits and the bat gauge explained"
excerpt: "Compare blade limits with gauge openings, and understand why passing through a larger opening is not enough."
status: draft
---

When an umpire slides a frame over a cricket bat, the object is a **bat gauge**. It checks the bat against a prescribed shape. The test is not a measurement of how far the batter can hit the ball.

The MCC rules require both compliance with the blade-size limits and passage through the gauge. Those are related requirements, not two alternative ways to make an oversized blade acceptable. [MCC 2026 Laws, Law 5.7 and Appendix B.8](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf)

## Blade limits and gauge openings are different numbers

| Dimension | Maximum blade measurement | Gauge aperture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Width | 10.8 cm | 11.0 cm |
| Depth | 6.7 cm | 6.8 cm |
| Edge | 4.0 cm | 4.1 cm |

The overall bat, including its inserted handle, must not exceed 96.52 cm in length. The gauge has a defined curved shape, so these aperture dimensions should not be treated as an arbitrary rectangular hole. [MCC Law 5.7 and Appendix B.8](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf)

Why is the opening slightly larger? The ECB explains that the extra space accommodates permitted covering, such as an anti-scuff front, and the gauge accommodates the traditionally curved bat face. It does not increase the permitted size of the underlying blade. [ECB explanation, 25 May 2018](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/uk/england-and-wales-cricket-board/pressreleases/cdc-penalises-yorkshire-ccc-over-players-bat-size-breach-in-second-xi-trophy-match-2519389)

For example, a bare blade measured at 10.9 cm wide exceeds the 10.8 cm limit. Pointing to an 11.0 cm gauge opening would not make that blade compliant. This is an illustrative comparison, not a finding about a player’s equipment.

## A visible check is not a finding of deliberate wrongdoing

Checking the bat actually brought to the crease is different from checking another bat beforehand. The visible check helps explain the interruption in play; it is not evidence, by itself, of deliberate wrongdoing.

## A failed check does not identify a universal penalty

Equipment standards and competition sanctions are separate questions. The ECB’s 2018 Yorkshire case, for example, involved a Second XI Trophy points deduction following a disciplinary hearing. That outcome belongs to that case and competition; it is not an IPL penalty template. [ECB disciplinary release](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/uk/england-and-wales-cricket-board/pressreleases/cdc-penalises-yorkshire-ccc-over-players-bat-size-breach-in-second-xi-trophy-match-2519389)

When an incident occurs, separate three facts: what was measured, what officials decided about that bat, and what the competition subsequently announced. A clip of the test cannot answer all three.

For players buying or checking equipment, the practical distinction is the same: use the legal blade limits, account for permitted coverings, and use the proper gauge. Do not substitute its larger opening dimensions for the blade specification.
